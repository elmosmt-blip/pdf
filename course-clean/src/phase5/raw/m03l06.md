## Acceptance basis
Treat the delivered stencil as a controlled part. Acceptance starts with a traceable measurement report naming the drawing revision, measurement method, operator, date and serial number.

Compare each reported value to the issued specification, not to a generic guideline. A report that omits a required characteristic, gives no tolerance band, or shows an unapproved deviation is held or rejected until the supplier supplies corrected data.

## Critical dimensional checks
Review foil thickness, aperture width and length, wall taper, area ratio, step depth, registration, tension and flatness, confirming both the reported value and the tolerance band for each. Where the specification is silent, take the design reference from IPC-7525C and the paste or equipment datasheet, then document the accepted limit before release.

## Paste release geometry
Area ratio and wall taper control release. A low area ratio, a poor taper or a damaged wall leaves paste in the aperture or smears it across the pad.

On small apertures, check the supplier's measured values against the paste datasheet and the printer capability. A nominal design value means little once the measured geometry is marginal.

## Thickness, step and volume control
Foil thickness sets paste volume, and step features give different volumes on one stencil. Confirm that thickness sits inside the issued tolerance and that step depth, location and transition angle match the drawing.

An error in either one produces too much or too little paste even when every aperture looks correct.

## Tension and flatness
Tension holds registration and board contact steady, so measure it after fabrication and periodically through service. Flatness governs contact across the print area.

Use the supplier's measurement method and the issued limit. Where the specification defines no limit, set a range from printer capability and paste process data, then verify it against your paste and equipment datasheet.

## Cleaning regime
Write the cleaning method, chemistry, temperature, cycle time, rinse and drying sequence into a controlled document. Ultrasonic, spray, immersion and wipe methods differ in aggressiveness and in what residue they remove.

Verify the regime against your paste and equipment datasheet, and inspect the apertures after cleaning before the stencil returns to service.

## Lifetime tracking and retirement
Track each stencil by serial number and log print cycles, paste exposure, cleaning cycles, cleaning method, inspection findings, tension, thickness, aperture wear and step condition.

Retire on measured drift, repeat defects or a failed inspection, not on a fixed cycle count alone. Use the trend data to set warning and reject limits for your own process.

## Key takeaways
Accept against the issued specification, including the measured area ratio, because dimensions in tolerance can still hide a release failure.
Record tension, flatness and step condition at acceptance and through life, since these drift before the apertures do.
Retire a stencil on measured trend, not on a cycle count someone picked before the program started.

## Common mistakes
Passing a report that omits the area ratio :: Require the characteristic on the report and hold the stencil until it is measured.
Treating a cleaning cycle as validated because it looks clean :: Inspect apertures under magnification and requalify the cycle after any chemistry or power change.
Retiring on cycle count alone :: Retire on tension, thickness and repeat-defect trend, and keep the records that show the drift.

## Glossary
Registration :: How accurately the aperture pattern aligns with the board pads during printing.
Tension :: The stretching force on the stencil mesh or foil, expressed in N/cm, that keeps the print area flat.
Step depth :: The local thickness difference built into a step stencil.
Stencil serial number :: The unique identifier that ties a stencil to its measurement report and lifetime history.
