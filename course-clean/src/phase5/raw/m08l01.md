## Why through-hole still matters
Through-hole technology still earns its place where a joint must resist mechanical load, hold a heavy connector, carry real current, or survive repeated insertion and removal. A plated through-hole gives the lead a barrel to wet into, and the solder inside that barrel adds retention and a thermal path.

The price is process control: hole diameter, plating thickness, lead diameter, flux activity, preheat and solder dwell all decide whether the hole fills and wets reliably.

## Finished plated-hole diameter controls
The drill size is not the final hole size. The finished plated-hole diameter is what remains after copper plating and the final finish.

For solder fill the relevant gap is the finished hole diameter minus the lead diameter. A board supplier may control drill diameter, plated diameter, plating thickness and wall coverage, and the assembly process still has to verify the finished diameter, because that dimension sets both capillary gap and heat transfer.

## Lead diameter and tolerance stack
Connector leads carry a nominal diameter and a tolerance, and the board hole carries its own. The worst-case clearance combines the smallest hole with the largest lead, and the largest clearance combines the largest hole with the smallest lead.

For common small through-hole leads, a diametral clearance of roughly 0.10 mm to 0.30 mm is often workable for wave or selective solder. The right value depends on paste or flux, preheat, solder temperature, dwell, board orientation and pad thermal mass, so verify it against your paste, flux and equipment datasheet.

## Clearance affects fill, not just fit
Too small a gap and flux and solder cannot penetrate, air stays trapped, and the joint shows incomplete fill or voids. Too large a gap and capillary action weakens, heat transfer to the hole falls off, and the barrel may never wet fully.

A radial clearance of roughly 0.05 mm to 0.15 mm is a practical starting range for many plated through-hole joints, confirmed by cross-section or X-ray on the actual board and component.

## Expected fill is a process result
Geometry gives an estimate; the fill height is a process result. The theoretical annular volume is the hole volume minus the lead volume over the hole depth.

Actual fill comes out lower for oxide, weak flux activity, low board temperature, short dwell, poor wall wetting or trapped air. Run a trial, measure the fill height, and use that measured fraction for prediction until the process is stable.

## Connectors, mechanical load and power
A connector joint may be through-hole for pull-out strength, shear resistance and durability under cable strain.

On a power path the current travels through the plated barrel and the solder joint, so plating quality, solder fill and thermal relief design all matter. Check current rating against the connector specification and the relevant IPC guidance for current carrying and thermal rise rather than inferring it from hole size.

## Verification before production
Before releasing a through-hole design, verify finished hole diameter, lead diameter, hole depth, plating thickness and pad geometry.

Then run a solderability trial and inspect cross-sections or X-rays for fill height, wetting, voiding and solder balling. Use IPC-A-610 acceptance for your product class as the pass or fail reference, and treat the calculated fill as a prediction that measurement has to confirm.

## Key takeaways
Use the finished plated-hole diameter, not the drill size, because plating and finish change the gap that governs fill.
Stack the lead and hole tolerances to both extremes, since the worst-case gap decides whether the process can fill at all.
Measure a trial fill fraction before predicting fill height, because the geometry calculation gives a ceiling rather than a result.

## Common mistakes
Calculating clearance from the drill diameter :: Use the finished plated diameter, which is smaller and is what the solder sees.
Quoting a single clearance figure as universal :: Check it against your flux, preheat and dwell, since the workable gap depends on all of them.
Treating calculated fill as achieved fill :: Measure the trial fill height and use that fraction until the process is stable.

## Glossary
Annular clearance :: The radial gap between the finished hole wall and the lead.
Fill height :: The height the solder reaches inside the plated hole, measured from the exit side.
Thermal relief :: A pad connection pattern that limits heat flow into or out of a through-hole pad.
Plating thickness :: The copper thickness on the hole wall, which sets current capacity and wall strength.
