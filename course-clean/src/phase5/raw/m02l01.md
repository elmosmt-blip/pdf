## Why moisture inside a package matters
Most plastic packages are built from hygroscopic materials: mold compound, die attach, underfill and polymer substrates. All of them take up water vapour from the air around them, and how much depends on package size, material composition, storage temperature, relative humidity and exposure time. Reflow then vaporises that water fast, raises internal pressure and loads weak interfaces.

The damage shows up as delamination, package cracking, die attach voiding, wire lift or intermittent electrical failures that surface long after the board passed test.

## How moisture enters the package
Water enters through the package body and through seams, exposed die surfaces and porous interfaces. Diffusion is slow at room temperature and quickens as temperature rises. A device in a dry cabinet or a sealed moisture barrier bag with desiccant stays below a critical internal moisture level; once the bag opens, absorption starts from the factory environment.

Packages do not absorb at the same rate. Thin, large or strongly hygroscopic packages react faster than thick, small ones.

## What happens during reflow
Reflow heats the package body and its interfaces quickly. Water that has diffused in vaporises and expands, and where the vapour pressure exceeds the strength of an interface, the interface separates. The industry calls it the popcorn effect.

Visible damage may be a crack or a lifted lead, but the same event can leave delamination at the die attach, the mold compound or the substrate interface with nothing to see. An electrical test can pass at that point and fail later under thermal cycling or humidity stress.

## MSL rating and floor life
The moisture sensitivity level classifies how quickly a device absorbs moisture under defined ambient conditions. Floor life is the total ambient exposure a device may take before it must be baked before reflow. Under IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033 the typical floor lives are MSL 1 unlimited, MSL 2 12 months, MSL 3 168 h, MSL 4 72 h, MSL 5 48 h, MSL 5a 24 h, and MSL 6 with no floor life, which makes the bake mandatory before use with the post-bake reflow window taken from the label.

These are package-level control limits, not predictions that a specific device will fail past them. They exist to stop moisture-related reflow damage before it starts.

## Reading markings and bag data
The MSL rating may sit on the device label, the moisture barrier bag, the tape and reel label or the package datasheet. A missing or unclear marking means you use the datasheet or treat the device as the most restrictive applicable level until someone verifies it.

Bag data should carry the MSL, the humidity indicator card status, the desiccant condition and the bag opening date. Card thresholds are card-specific, so check the card datasheet and IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033. A card past its threshold says the internal environment is no longer acceptable for direct reflow, even when the calculated floor life has time left.

## Using reflow history
Floor life is cumulative ambient exposure, not time since the last bag opening. Under IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033 a qualifying reflow completed inside the remaining floor life resets it. Floor life that expired before reflow means baking before reflow, and a humidity indicator card past its threshold means baking regardless of the remaining floor life.

Some programs also cap the number of reflows or require special handling after several passes. Check the package qualification, the customer procedure and IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033 rather than assuming.

## Practical storage and floor-life control
Control starts with dry storage, sealed moisture barrier bags, desiccant and humidity indicator cards. A dry cabinet often runs at 5 % to 10 % RH, but only the 5 % RH or less band suspends the floor-life clock under J-STD-033 — a 10 % cabinet is comfortable and earns no credit. Confirm the setpoint against your equipment datasheet.

Check bake conditions, cabinet setpoints and reflow profiles against IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033, the package datasheet and your paste and equipment datasheets. Track bag opening time, card status, reflow time, bake time and any reflow count limit. When the next reflow will not happen inside the remaining floor life, bake first; visual inspection and electrical test will not tell you whether the package is dry.

## Key takeaways
Absorbed moisture turns to vapour in reflow, so the failure mechanism is internal and often invisible to electrical test.
Treat floor life as cumulative ambient exposure, and reset it only through a qualifying reflow or an accepted bake.
A humidity indicator card past its threshold forces a bake even when the calculated floor life still has hours left.

## Common mistakes
Counting floor life from receipt or from the last reflow without checking the procedure :: Count from the bag opening, and treat a reflow as a reset only where IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033 and your procedure allow it.
Relying on a 10 % RH dry cabinet to suspend floor life :: Use the 5 % RH or less band for suspension, and record the setpoint you actually ran.
Releasing a device that passed electrical test after moisture exposure :: Inspect for delamination and bake the lot, because the electrical result does not show internal separation.

## Glossary
Popcorn effect :: Package cracking or interface separation caused by moisture vaporising during reflow.
Moisture sensitivity level :: The J-STD-020 classification of how much ambient exposure a non-hermetic package tolerates before reflow.
Floor life :: The ambient exposure a moisture-sensitive device may accumulate before it must be baked.
Delamination :: Separation between layers inside a package, such as mold compound and die or substrate.
