## Why selective soldering is chosen
Selective soldering serves mixed assemblies where through-hole joints cannot go through a full wave without damaging nearby surface-mount parts, plastic connectors or heat-sensitive devices.

The process localises heat and flux to one joint group, so neighbours stay protected while the joint stays repeatable. The choice is a joint geometry decision rather than a question of machine availability.

The group must be reachable from the bottom side, have clearance for the nozzle, and let flux reach the joint without being absorbed by adjacent paste or trapped under a component. Agree the IPC-A-610 acceptance class before the trial, because the same visual defect passes in one class and fails in another. Confirm too that the process brings no new risk of its own: flux migration, solder balls, or thermal damage to neighbouring joints.

## Joint groups and geometry
A joint group is a set of joints served by one nozzle path, one flux application and one thermal profile. Isolated pins, connector rows, mixed through-hole and surface-mount pads, and repair zones are typical.

Geometry decides the choice: pin diameter, hole plating, pad size, standoff height, body height and the distance to the nearest heat-sensitive part tell you whether a mini-wave, dip or jet nozzle can reach the joint. A tall connector may need a taller nozzle or a different flux delivery method, and a low-standoff part may need a shallow wave to avoid flooding.

Check against the board layout and nozzle datasheet before committing to a path. Where a group mixes technologies, confirm that surface-mount joints are already reflowed or that the selective process will not disturb them.

## Nozzle selection
Nozzle choice balances wetting energy, thermal exposure and reach.

A mini-wave makes a small solder wave and suits exposed through-hole pins and mixed joints. A dip nozzle gives more uniform immersion for small groups and raises the heat seen by nearby pads. Jet or spray-assisted nozzles improve flux delivery and wetting on tight pitch and demand control of splash and solder mist.

With heat-sensitive neighbours, take the smallest nozzle that still wets completely, and verify nozzle height, wave height and dwell against the equipment datasheet. Record practice values such as orifice size, wave height and standoff as a range until capability is established, and keep the nozzle compatible with the alloy and flux chemistry.

## Flux strategy
Flux strategy has to match joint geometry and contamination level.

Clean boards with plated through-holes may need only a low-solids liquid flux applied by controlled spray or jet. Oxidised pins, high-thermal-mass connectors or joints with limited access may need higher activity or a pre-applied flux preform, with residue and corrosion risk evaluated first.

Give enough flux to wet the joint and the wall without migrating to nearby surface-mount joints or throwing solder balls. With aerosol or ultrasonic delivery, verify droplet size, pressure and solids against the paste and flux datasheets, because the same nozzle behaves differently with another chemistry. Check flux placement visually and by cross-section before release.

## Heat-sensitive neighbours
Neighbours are the reason for the process, so the thermal budget has to be controlled at the joint rather than at the nozzle.

Preheat should bring board and joint to a stable temperature before solder contact, which cuts thermal shock and improves wetting without a long dwell. Set peak and dwell from the component moisture sensitivity level and the alloy liquidus, with a margin agreed by the design and reliability teams.

Measure the surface temperature at the neighbour's body or pin, not only the pot temperature, and check a plastic connector, electrolytic capacitor or small-signal device against its datasheet and the IPC J-STD-001 thermal exposure guidance. Profile the worst-case joint and the nearest sensitive component together.

## Throughput and cycle time
Throughput follows the number of joint groups, path length, dwell, flux application time and preheat stabilisation.

A single group runs quickly, while a mixed assembly often needs several passes, nozzle changes or flux applications. Model cycle time as fixed plus variable elements: flux application, preheat, solder dwell, cooling and a rework allowance.

When throughput is short, cut unnecessary path length or combine compatible groups first, then adjust dwell and flux within the wetting window. Never shorten dwell below the point where fill and wetting hold unless a qualified alternate process is approved. Confirm the joint against IPC-A-610 and guide any rework by IPC-7711/7721.

## Decision method
A defensible decision starts with geometry. Draw the joint group, mark the nozzle access path, and identify the nearest heat-sensitive component.

Then choose the nozzle that reaches the joint with the least thermal exposure, and the flux that wets without migrating. A low-standoff joint near a plastic connector suits a shallow mini-wave with low-solids flux. A high-thermal-mass connector with oxidised pins suits a dip nozzle, higher activity and longer preheat. Tight-pitch mixed joints often want a jet-assisted nozzle with controlled flux volume.

Defend the choice by showing that geometry, neighbour clearance and thermal budget fit the nozzle and flux, and back it with trial data rather than vendor claims.

## Verification and documentation
Verify with cross-sections, hole fill, wetting angle, residue inspection and thermal profiling at both the joint and the neighbour.

Record nozzle type, nozzle height, wave height, dwell, preheat, peak temperature, flux type, flux volume and spray parameters. Use a small trial matrix to find the window, then confirm on production samples.

State practice-based values as ranges and verify them against the paste, flux and equipment datasheets. Accept against the agreed IPC-A-610 class and the IPC J-STD-001 workmanship requirements. Rework a failed joint per IPC-7711/7721 and update the window when the failure shows a systematic nozzle or flux mismatch, so the documentation lets another engineer repeat the choice.

## Key takeaways
Choose selective soldering from joint geometry and neighbour clearance, not from machine availability.
Take the smallest nozzle that wets completely, because thermal exposure to the neighbour is the constraint being managed.
Profile the nearest sensitive component as well as the joint, since the neighbour sets the ceiling.

## Common mistakes
Selecting a dip nozzle for the whole group because it wets quickly :: Check neighbour temperature first, since the extra heat is what damages it.
Raising flux activity without checking residue :: Verify the residue class and cleaning plan, or corrosion risk replaces the wetting problem.
Shortening dwell to meet takt time :: Confirm fill and wetting on a cross-section before accepting the faster cycle.

## Glossary
Joint group :: A set of joints processed with one nozzle path, flux application and thermal profile.
Mini-wave :: A small localised solder wave formed by a nozzle, used for selective joints.
Standoff :: The gap between the component body and the board surface.
Flux migration :: Flux travelling away from the intended joint onto neighbouring pads or components.
