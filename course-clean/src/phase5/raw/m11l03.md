## Rework scope and thermal budget
Rework is a controlled thermal operation, not a repair shortcut.

Before starting, define the thermal budget: preheat temperature, peak temperature measured at the joint, time above liquidus, dwell, cooling behaviour, and the number of thermal cycles the board and component have already seen.

The budget must suit the laminate, the surface finish, the moisture sensitivity level of the component and the paste or solder-ball material. Take the assembly document, the board supplier guidance, the component datasheet and the paste or ball datasheet as the controlling references.

Where the allowed cycle count is not stated, treat any additional cycle as a deviation that needs engineering approval.

## Preheat and hot-air setup
Preheat softens the thermal shock and lets the joint reach reflow temperature without an extreme air temperature.

Measure board temperature near the component rather than trusting the air setting. A workable practice preheats the board to a measured temperature that supports paste activation and ball collapse while the component body stays inside its rated limit, verified against the paste, component and equipment datasheets.

Use a nozzle that covers the footprint without blasting neighbours. On a large board or a thermally massive part, raise preheat or fit a larger nozzle rather than raising peak air temperature.

## Paste-in-pad and replacement materials
Paste-in-pad suits QFN, MLF and some small land-pattern replacements where design and process allow it.

The paste must be compatible with the existing finish and reflow profile, and the deposit volume controlled by stencil, syringe or dispenser. For BGA replacement, paste-in-pad is less common than new solder balls or a pre-balled component, though a qualified repair method may use it.

Clean the pads, avoid contamination, and confirm paste lot, expiry and storage. IPC J-STD-001 gives the general soldering requirements; apply the qualified method and record the material.

## BGA and QFN removal
During removal, heat the joint until the solder collapses, then lift with minimal force. Never pry a component that solid solder still locks in place.

On a BGA, watch for warpage, lifted pads and disturbed neighbours; on a QFN, watch thermal-pad adhesion and corner lifting. If the part will not release, improve preheat or heat distribution before raising peak temperature.

After removal, inspect the pads under magnification and clean residual solder with a qualified method. IPC-7530 gives rework, modification and repair guidance, so use it together with your process qualification and work instruction.

## Replacement, alignment and reflow
Align the replacement with fiducials, land patterns or a placement aid.

Alignment matters most on a BGA because the joints are hidden: use a qualified placement method and verify with X-ray after reflow. On a QFN, check coplanarity, paste volume and thermal-pad wetting.

Reflow on a measured profile that meets the paste or ball specification and the board thermal limits. Where the component is moisture-sensitive, follow IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033 and record floor-life status before the rework begins.

## Inspection and documentation
Inspect after rework by the method the assembly calls for: magnification on QFN and passives, X-ray on BGA and hidden joints, and functional test where specified.

IPC-A-610 gives visual acceptance criteria, applied at the class and criteria the contract defines. Record the rework as a process step, not as an undocumented exception.

The record carries operator, date, station, nozzle, preheat setpoint, measured peak temperature, dwell, cycle count, paste or ball lot, flux lot, inspection result and any deviation.

## Allowed cycles and budget control
Every thermal exposure spends part of the component and the board budget. Count cycles per component and per board area, because neighbours see the heat too.

If a component needs several attempts, stop and decide whether the failure is alignment, paste volume, contamination, pad damage or an unsuitable profile. Repeated hot-air attempts without a new root cause lift pads, delaminate the board or damage the part.

Where the contract or board specification gives a maximum cycle count, do not exceed it without engineering approval.

## Key takeaways
Count cycles per component and per area before the hot air goes on, because the budget is spent in exposures and not in minutes.
Measure peak temperature at the joint, since the controller display is the air temperature and the joint is what the specification limits.
Raise preheat or nozzle size instead of air temperature, because extra air heat warps the board and blasts the neighbours.

## Common mistakes
Prying a component that has not fully collapsed :: Keep heating until the solder is liquid, because force lifts pads and cracks the part.
Trusting the hot-air setpoint as the joint temperature :: Profile with a thermocouple at the joint, since air and joint differ by a wide margin.
Repeating hot-air attempts when the first one failed :: Stop and find the root cause, because each attempt spends the cycle budget.

## Glossary
Paste-in-pad :: A repair method that prints or dispenses paste onto the land pattern before placing the component.
Thermal cycle :: One complete heating and cooling exposure counted against the allowed rework budget.
Collapse :: The stage at which a solder ball melts and settles onto its pad during reflow.
Warpage :: Bending of the board under heat that can tilt a component or open joints.
