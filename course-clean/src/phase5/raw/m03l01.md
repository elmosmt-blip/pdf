## Paste volume is a joint requirement
Define the joint before drawing the aperture: pad geometry, component termination, target standoff, acceptable fillet, and the paste volume needed to wet the termination and form a reliable joint. The aperture then has to deliver that volume while staying inside the pad, so reflow creates no bridge.

Stencil thickness, paste rheology, print speed, squeegee pressure, separation speed and cleaning cycle all decide how much paste actually transfers. Treat the aperture as a volume source and the print process as the transfer mechanism between them.

## Area ratio compares contact area with wall adhesion
Area ratio is the aperture area divided by the sidewall area of the stencil wall. For a rectangular aperture the sidewall area is the perimeter times the stencil thickness, so area ratio equals length times width divided by perimeter times thickness.

A higher ratio gives the paste a larger bottom contact area against the wall that holds it in the aperture. IPC-7525 stencil design guidelines are the common reference for release indicators, with a working target of about 0.66 or above; confirm that against your paste and equipment datasheet.

## Aspect ratio describes depth against width
Aspect ratio is aperture width divided by stencil thickness, and for rectangular apertures you take the smaller dimension, because that dimension controls the narrowest release path. A low ratio means a deep, narrow hole where paste clings to the sidewalls and fails to release cleanly.

IPC-7525 again supplies the release indicator, with a common working target above about 1.5. Fine-pitch designs often fall below it and need a process or paste change rather than a longer argument.

## Deposit volume is nominal volume times transfer efficiency
Nominal deposit volume is aperture area multiplied by stencil thickness. Expected deposit volume multiplies that by transfer efficiency, which absorbs paste left in the aperture, paste smeared during separation, and paste never released at all.

Transfer efficiency depends on the process, so measure it or take it from paste and equipment data. Where planning needs a single number, use a conservative value from the paste datasheet or a recent print trial.

## Set the joint volume target first
Estimate the required paste volume from pad area, desired deposit height, component termination geometry and paste manufacturer guidance. On chip components the deposit has to wet the termination and form a fillet without giving adjacent pads enough paste to bridge in reflow.

Larger pads leave more room: adjust volume through aperture area, stencil thickness or a step stencil. Compare the calculated expected deposit volume with the required volume before committing to the aperture.

## Geometry changes that improve release
When area ratio or aspect ratio comes out low, reduce stencil thickness, enlarge the aperture within the pad, choose a paste with better release, or change the print parameters. Electropolished walls, laser-cut shape, wall taper and step stencils also help.

Every one of those trades volume against release and bridging risk, so judge them on print trials and inspection data rather than on a single ratio.

## Decision rule for release adequacy
Calculate area ratio, aspect ratio, nominal volume and expected deposit volume. Ratios that meet the release indicators from your applicable design guideline, together with an expected volume that meets the joint requirement, make adequate release likely.

A low ratio means the aperture may release badly even when nominal volume looks generous. A low volume means the joint may be underfilled even when release is good. Document the assumptions and confirm with paste deposit measurement.

## Key takeaways
Set the joint volume target before the aperture exists, then check that the aperture can deliver it inside the pad.
Judge release on both area ratio and aspect ratio, because a deep narrow aperture can pass one and fail the other.
Take transfer efficiency from measurement or the paste datasheet, since a generic factor hides the process you actually run.

## Common mistakes
Designing the aperture and checking the volume afterwards :: Fix the joint volume first and size the aperture against it.
Using a single release ratio as a pass or fail verdict :: Combine both ratios with SPI data and the paste datasheet before releasing the design.
Assuming nominal volume equals deposit volume :: Multiply by a measured transfer efficiency and compare with the joint requirement.

## Glossary
Area ratio :: Aperture opening area divided by the stencil sidewall area, the usual indicator of paste release.
Aspect ratio :: Aperture width divided by stencil thickness, taken on the narrowest dimension of a rectangular aperture.
Transfer efficiency :: The share of nominal aperture volume that reaches the pad as a printed deposit.
Step stencil :: A stencil with locally reduced thickness that lowers paste volume in selected areas.
