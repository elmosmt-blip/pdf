## What an SPI report is trying to answer
A solder paste inspection report is a measurement, not only a screen. It shows how much paste was deposited, where, and how consistently: volume percentage against nominal, paste height, paste area, placement offset, histograms, and trend over time.

The engineer reads those metrics to infer the dominant print defect and trace it to a process cause. Take the acceptability limits from the product specification and the relevant IPC-A-610 class, then check them against the paste, stencil and printer datasheets.

## Volume percentage as the first clue
Volume percentage compares measured paste volume with the nominal volume expected from aperture area, stencil thickness and paste density. Low volume usually means under-fill, incomplete transfer or paste lost during release. High volume means excess paste, overfilling or accumulation from poor cleaning.

Volume alone misleads, because a pad can carry the right volume in the wrong shape, height or place. Read it with height, area and offset.

## Height and area separate under-fill from spreading
Low height with low area supports under-fill or incomplete transfer. Low height with high area suggests smearing or slumping, where paste spread sideways and lost height. High height with high area points to excess paste and bridging risk, and high height with low area to poor release, paste buildup or a local stencil problem.

Process windows vary with paste and stencil design, so check them against your own paste datasheet and design rules.

## Offset and skew
Offset measures how far the paste centroid sits from the pad centre. Skew is a pattern of consistent directional displacement across pads, components or panel locations.

A random offset pattern points to board support, conveyor handling or vision instability. A consistent direction points to stencil alignment, board seating, support pin interference or a systematic vision offset. Where offset is small but area and height are normal, the dominant issue is registration rather than deposition.

## Histograms: uniform or localised
A histogram counts measurements in each range of volume, height, area or offset. Narrow and near target means stable printing.

A broad histogram means instability: viscosity drift, cleaning variation, inconsistent squeegee pressure. Shifted low means under-fill or incomplete transfer; shifted high means excess paste or accumulation. A bimodal shape means two populations are being measured together, such as one panel side printing differently from the other, or one stencil area behaving differently.

## Trend data: stable defect or drift
Trends distinguish stable, drifting, stepping and oscillating behaviour. A stable low-volume trend suggests a design or setup issue — aperture size, stencil thickness, paste rheology. A gradual drift toward low volume suggests paste drying, stencil clogging or degrading cleaning. A step change follows an event such as a paste lot, stencil, squeegee or program change. Oscillation points to cleaning frequency, board support variation or conveyor handling.

Trend data decides whether you face a fixed defect mode or a process drift, and the two need different responses.

## Naming the dominant defect mode
The dominant mode is the one that explains the combined pattern. Under-fill shows low volume, low height and low area. Excess shows high volume, high height and high area. Bridging shows high volume or area between adjacent pads, especially at fine pitch. Skew shows normal volume and shape with a consistent offset. Smearing shows low height with an elongated or irregular area, and slumping shows low height with a widened area and poor edge definition.

The likely cause then follows from whether the pattern is uniform, localised, directional, drifting or step-like.

## Key takeaways
Read the whole report before naming a defect, because volume, height, area and offset each rule alternative causes in or out.
Use the histogram to see whether one population or two are printing, and split by panel side or stencil zone when it is bimodal.
Separate a stable defect from a drift in the trend, since a design fix does not cure a degrading stencil.

## Common mistakes
Diagnosing smearing from a low volume percentage alone :: Confirm height and area, since smearing shows low height with widened area rather than low area.
Ignoring offset because volume is acceptable :: Check the offset trend, because registration drift produces defects the volume screen cannot see.
Treating an oscillating trend as random :: Look for cleaning frequency and board support variation before changing print parameters.

## Glossary
Volume percentage :: Measured deposit volume as a percentage of the nominal aperture volume.
Skew :: A consistent directional displacement of deposits across pads or panel locations.
Under-fill :: A deposit that is short in volume, height and area because the aperture did not fill or release.
Slumping :: Lateral flow of the deposit after release, producing a wider, flatter print.
