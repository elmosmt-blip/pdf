## Floor life is a clock, not a label
A moisture-sensitive device starts consuming floor life when its moisture barrier bag opens and the device meets ambient air. The clock runs under the moisture sensitivity level assigned by IPC/JEDEC J-STD-020 and the handling rules of IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033, which defines floor life at reference conditions of 30 °C and 60 % RH — so a hot, humid stores area burns the clock faster than a controlled dry cabinet.

An expired clock does not make the batch scrap. It makes it a process deviation that has to be contained before reflow. The first questions are whether the exposure history is known, whether the remaining floor life covers the planned reflow, and whether the package type can be baked safely.

## Deciding whether the batch must be baked
Compare elapsed exposure with the floor life for that level in IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033. Where elapsed time exceeds the allowance and reflow is planned, bake before reflow unless the manufacturer provides a documented exception.

Dry storage under J-STD-033 targets 5 % RH or less. A cabinet running above that leaves the devices exposed, so recalculate floor life rather than assuming storage protected them. Devices at the lowest moisture sensitivity level under J-STD-020 usually need no bake unless contamination, handling damage or a customer requirement says otherwise. Where floor life remains and dry storage is compliant, the batch may proceed, but only with a complete storage record.

## Bake conditions by package type
IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033 indexes bake conditions to the carrier, the package body thickness, the moisture sensitivity level and the maximum temperature the component allows.

For loose parts in trays, tubes or waffle packs whose carriers and components are rated for it, the 125 °C column of Table 4-1 applies: 5 to 16 h for a body up to 1.4 mm, 18 to 48 h for 1.4 to 2.0 mm and 48 h for 2.0 to 4.5 mm, with the longer times at higher MSL and when floor life was exceeded by more than 72 h.

The 24 h figure sometimes quoted for a 125 °C bake is the supplier dry-pack condition from Table 4-2, not a user bake, so it must not become a production recipe. For tape and reel, J-STD-033 provides the lower-temperature long bake at 40 °C — 8 to 22 days for a body up to 1.4 mm and 25 to 79 days for 1.4 to 2.0 mm, by MSL — because the tape, reel, labels and cover tape cannot take more heat. Cumulative bake time above 90 °C is limited to 96 h unless the supplier states otherwise.

Justify the condition by package type: polymer tape and reels deform, labels lift and cover tape shifts at high temperature. Check the chosen condition against the component datasheet, the carrier datasheet and the oven capability.

## Dry storage after bake
After baking, store the batch at 5 % RH or less under J-STD-033, or repack it in a moisture barrier bag with desiccant and a humidity indicator card. The floor-life clock resets only when bake, dry storage and packaging conditions are met and documented; otherwise the original exposure stays in the batch history.

Dry cabinets need calibrated humidity sensors and logged RH, not only a setpoint. If RH climbs above target, the exposure clock may resume, or the prior exposure stays unresolved. Check practice-based setpoints, alarm limits and bake durations against your equipment datasheet and your moisture control procedure.

## Containing a moisture breach
When expired floor life turns up mid-lot, stop feeding the affected reels, trays or tubes and quarantine every device from the same lot, date code, vacuum bag, dry cabinet and handling window. Nothing from that batch reflows until the bake decision is made and documented.

Record the MSL, lot code, date code, reel or serial identifiers, open time, elapsed floor life, dry cabinet RH, temperature, storage location and the planned reflow time. Where some devices already reflowed, isolate the boards and assess them by electrical test, X-ray, cross-section or failure analysis. Keep containment wide enough to cover commingled lots and incomplete records.

## Containing an ESD breach
Where an ESD control failure turns up in storage or staging, quarantine the devices and boards in the EPA for the breach window. ANSI/ESD S20.20 and IEC 61340-5-1 require an EPA with grounded personnel, ESD protective packaging, verified work surfaces and controlled handling.

Check wrist strap logs, floor and surface resistance records, ionizer status, packaging type and operator training. ESD damage is often latent, so visual inspection may show nothing. If the breach surfaces after assembly, trace every board handled in the EPA from the last verified control check to the moment of discovery, and widen the range where records are incomplete.

## Defining the traceability range
The range covers all units sharing the lot or date code, storage location, dry cabinet, vacuum bag, shift, feeder, work order, reflow oven and reflow profile. For an ESD breach, add every board handled in the EPA during the breach window, plus feeders, magazines, conveyors, bins, packaging and any adjacent lots that may have been commingled.

Where the storage system uses bins without full serial control, widen the range to the whole bin, lot or shift. The aim is a population bounded by evidence, not the smallest population you can argue for.

## Line decision and documentation
Three outcomes cover most cases: bake and repack where the package type allows, scrap where baking is not allowed or the package is damaged, or release only after the bake record, dry storage record and traceability disposition are complete.

Document the deviation, containment, bake condition, dry storage RH, reflow qualification and final disposition. Check practice-based values such as cabinet alarm limits, bake loading density and reflow scheduling windows against your equipment datasheet and process control plan. The decision should stand on the exposure record, the package type and the reflow plan.

## Key takeaways
Treat an expired floor life as a contained deviation, not as scrap and not as a label problem.
Check the carrier before choosing the bake, because tape and reel caps the bake at 40 °C and turns hours into days.
Bound the traceability range by evidence — lot, bag, cabinet, shift, feeder, oven — and widen it whenever records are incomplete.

## Common mistakes
Baking a tape-and-reel lot at 125 °C to save time :: Keep reels at or below 40 °C or de-reel and bake loose parts to a qualified recipe.
Releasing the batch after a 7 % RH cabinet reading :: Treat anything above 5 % RH as exposure and recalculate floor life before reflow.
Limiting containment to the two reels on the feeder :: Include the whole lot, bag, cabinet and shift, and widen further where records cannot prove otherwise.

## Glossary
Floor-life deviation :: A batch whose recorded exposure exceeds the allowed floor life before reflow.
Containment :: Action that stops suspect material from moving further into the process while the disposition is decided.
Latent ESD failure :: Damage that leaves a part working at test and failing later in service.
Traceability range :: The set of units, lots and handling records that the evidence shows to be affected by an exposure.
