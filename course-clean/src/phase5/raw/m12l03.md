## Cleanliness verification at the coating and ESD stage
Cleanliness verification asks whether residues from soldering, cleaning, handling or coating can compromise adhesion, surface insulation resistance, corrosion resistance or ESD protection.

At this stage the board may be inspected before conformal coating, after cleaning, and again after coating where the coating is expected to protect an ESD path. The result is a comparison against a defined criterion, never a measurement on its own: a pass means the measured contamination is below the limit for the tested area and the visual condition is acceptable, and a fail means the area is reworked, retested or dispositioned by engineering.

Write the criterion before testing, and state the method, the sampled area, the reporting units and the action on failure.

## Sampling and reporting
A cleanliness result means something only when the sampled area, extraction method, blank correction and reporting units are known.

ROSE and ion chromatography usually normalise to area, such as micrograms of sodium chloride equivalent per square centimetre or micrograms of a specific ion per square centimetre, and the tested area must match the area the result represents. Test a small high-risk area and the result applies to that area, not to the whole board.

Blank correction removes what the solvent, container and handling contributed. For a rework decision, record method, area, instrument, date, operator, visible defects, whether the sample was destructive, whether the area was masked, and whether the result was averaged.

## ROSE interpretation
ROSE extracts residues into a solvent and measures conductivity or resistivity.

It is fast and useful for process control, and it does not identify the ion, does not measure non-ionic residue and can be disturbed by weakly ionic or volatile material. Compare the result with the limit in the customer or internal process specification: above the limit the tested area fails; below it the area may pass for ionic contamination while visual inspection and ion-specific testing are still required on a high-reliability, fine-pitch or to-be-coated assembly.

ROSE is most useful when the process is stable and the same solvent, temperature and extraction time are used. A shift without a process change sends you to the blank, the solvent, the cell calibration and the sampling technique.

## Ion chromatography interpretation
Ion chromatography separates and quantifies individual ions such as chloride, sulfate, nitrate, acetate and formate.

It is used when ROSE fails, when a specific flux chemistry is suspected, or when corrosion risk has to be understood. Interpretation needs individual ion limits and, where specified, a total ionic limit, because a total can pass while one ion fails and the reverse can also happen.

The laboratory reports detection limits, and a value below detection is not zero. For rework, the failed ion and the process that put it there guide the cleaning: chloride usually points to paste, water or handling, and organic ions point to flux residue. Ion chromatography is slower than ROSE, so it serves qualification, failure analysis and periodic verification rather than every board.

## Particle count interpretation
Particle count verifies that particulate contamination is controlled before coating and inside the ESD-controlled area.

Airborne counts are compared with cleanroom classes from the ISO cleanroom particle count standard, and surface counts come from wiping, flushing or a counter over a defined area.

The criterion states the particle size threshold, the area or volume sampled and the maximum count. A particle count proves nothing about chemical cleanliness: it shows only that particles above the selected size are inside the allowance. For coating, particles cause pinholes, poor adhesion or localised corrosion where they hold moisture; on ESD surfaces they interrupt coating continuity or create high-resistance spots. Sample location matters, because a cleanroom can pass while the board surface fails.

## Visual inspection interpretation
Visual inspection finds residue, flux staining, corrosion, handling marks, coating defects and particles large enough to see.

It runs under controlled lighting and magnification against the IPC visual acceptability standard or a customer-approved visual standard. A visual pass means no unacceptable residue or defect is present in the inspected area.

Visual inspection cannot find all ionic contamination, so it is combined with chemical tests where the risk is high. A visible defect fails the area even when chemical tests pass, unless engineering issues a documented deviation. Look under connectors, near stencil apertures and along board edges where cleaning solution struggles to reach, and keep photographs and annotated drawings for marginal results and retests.

## Pass, rework and retest
The pass decision uses the most restrictive applicable criterion: customer specification, internal specification or standard method guidance.

A common sequence is visual pass, ROSE pass, ion chromatography pass and particle count pass. When any test fails, rework the area with a validated cleaning process and retest the same or a representative area. On a marginal result, repeat the test and check sampling technique, blank and calibration before accepting.

For coating and ESD work, ask whether residue could be trapped under the coating or shift surface resistivity; if so, require ion-specific confirmation before coating. The disposition states accepted, reworked, scrapped or released under deviation, and the retest is compared with the original criterion, not with a relaxed limit chosen after the fact.

## Where the acceptance numbers come from
A cleanliness criterion is never invented at the bench: it comes from the customer specification, the reliability plan or a published reference.

The most widely used reference is 1.56 microgram NaCl equivalent per square centimetre, reported per IPC-TM-650 method 2.3.25 and historically applied to assembled boards through IPC J-STD-001 and to bare boards through IPC-6012. Many high-reliability customers tighten it: about 1.0 microgram per square centimetre for high-impedance analogue and telecom hardware, about 0.75 for aerospace and military, and about 0.5 for medical assemblies, with ion chromatography per IPC-TM-650 method 2.3.28 confirming the specific ions.

Two cautions follow. ROSE reports a conductivity equivalent rather than a named ion, so two boards with the same ROSE value can carry very different corrosion risk. And a limit taken from another product family is a starting point for discussion, not an acceptance criterion.

## Key takeaways
Write the criterion before the test, because a limit chosen after the result is a rationalisation.
Report the sampled area with the number, since a localised pocket disappears inside a whole-board average.
Treat a marginal result as a reason to check the method, not as a reason to relax the limit.

## Common mistakes
Comparing ROSE readings from different solvents and extraction times :: Keep solvent, temperature and time fixed, because the value is only comparable within one method.
Reading a below-detection ion result as zero :: Treat it as below the reporting limit, since the laboratory cannot resolve less.
Accepting a board because the chemical tests passed while a visible defect remains :: Fail the area, because a documented deviation is the only route past it.

## Glossary
Blank correction :: Subtracting contamination contributed by solvent, container and handling from the measured result.
Ion chromatography :: A method that separates and quantifies individual ionic species in an extract.
Particle count :: The number of particles above a stated size in a defined area or volume.
Detection limit :: The smallest value an analytical method can report as distinguishable from background.
