## Selecting a coating for mixed assemblies
Coating selection starts with the service environment and the thermal, chemical and electrical requirements of the assembly.

Acrylics are easy to remove and repair, urethane gives good abrasion and chemical resistance, silicone tolerates high temperature and humidity, epoxy gives strong mechanical protection at the cost of removability, and parylene gives thin uniform coverage at the cost of vacuum deposition and careful masking.

On a mixed SMT and through-hole board, verify that the coating does not attack flux residue, potting compound, connectors or test points. IPC-CC-830 is the qualification standard to check against.

## Spray coating
Spray coating is common at low and medium volume and on assemblies with complex topography.

Typical spray viscosity is adjusted to the equipment range, often around 10 to 100 mPa·s for air-assisted systems, with the exact value taken from the coating supplier's process window and the spray equipment manual.

Atomisation pressure, nozzle size, travel speed and distance set the film thickness and the overspray. On mixed assemblies, keep high pressure away from small components, exposed vias, connectors and test pads. Measure wet film with a comb or gauge, then confirm dry film after cure; target values are commonly 25 to 130 µm depending on the coating class and IPC-CC-830 qualification, but the final range should match the qualified coating system.

## Dip coating
Dip coating gives good coverage on through-hole assemblies and boards with open component spacing.

The board is immersed and withdrawn at a controlled rate, commonly 100 to 300 mm/min, with dwell adjusted so the film drains without bridging. Viscosity, temperature and withdrawal speed set the wet film thickness; a typical process may aim for 50 to 150 µm dry film thickness, verified against the coating datasheet and IPC-CC-830 qualification.

Dip coating traps solvent under large components and bottom-terminated packages, so preheat, dwell and ventilation are controlled to limit voids and skips.

## Selective coating
Selective coating protects only the areas that need it, such as high-voltage regions, exposed copper or moisture-sensitive areas, while connectors, test points, heatsinks and mechanical interfaces stay bare.

Programmed dispensing or jetting places coating on defined areas, with nozzle path, coating volume, cure schedule and edge definition as the controls.

On mixed boards it cuts masking labour and keeps repair access, and it needs good CAD or Gerber data plus verification of component height, pin spacing and connector sealing. Confirm that the coating does not creep into anything it should not reach.

## Masking and cleaning compatibility
Masking protects connectors, test pads, heatsink interfaces, potting areas and repair zones.

Common masks are high-temperature tape, silicone caps, liquid photoimageable mask, and removable wax or polymer masks. Mask selection depends on the coating solvent, the cure temperature, the dwell and the cleaning chemistry: a solvent-based coating may attack some tapes, and a high-temperature cure may lift adhesive or leave residue.

Verify mask compatibility by running a coupon through the full coating, cure and cleaning sequence, and confirm the cleaning chemistry does not attack the mask either.

## Thickness control and cure verification
Dry film thickness drives insulation, mechanical protection, thermal behaviour and repairability.

Too thin a coating may not meet IPC-CC-830 qualification or IPC-A-610 acceptability for the intended class; too thick a coating traps solvent, cracks in thermal cycling or interferes with connectors and test probes.

Measure wet film before cure and dry film after, on flat areas, at edges and near components. Verify cure by the supplier's method: solvent rub, hardness, adhesion, or FTIR conversion where applicable. On heat-sensitive assemblies the cure temperature is the constraint that decides the chemistry.

## Repairability and rework
Repairability depends on the removal method, the access to the joints and the thermal exposure during rework.

Acrylics come off with solvent or mechanical action, while urethane, silicone and epoxy may need stronger solvents, abrasion or thermal assistance, and parylene may need plasma or specialist removal.

For rework and repair, follow IPC-7711 and IPC-7721 for acceptable methods, including preheat, temperature control and post-rework inspection. Leave enough uncoated area for probe access, soldering and inspection.

## Compatibility with the assembly flow
A coating strategy has to survive the whole flow: soldering, cleaning, inspection, functional test and repair.

Residual flux, ionic contamination, moisture or dust under the coating cause corrosion, insulation failure or adhesion loss, so cleaning before coating is validated against laminate, solder mask and component materials, and the board is dry before coating.

After coating, inspect for pinholes, bridging, voids, peeling, contamination and coverage of exposed copper, using IPC-A-610 acceptance criteria at the applicable class. At connectors and test points, confirm the masking held.

## Key takeaways
Pick the chemistry from the service environment and the rework method together, because a coating that cannot be opened turns a repair into a scrap.
Measure wet film before cure and dry film after, since thickness is the property the specifications judge.
Prove mask and coating compatibility on a coupon, because the full sequence reveals attacks a single step hides.

## Common mistakes
Spraying at high pressure over small components and vias :: Reduce pressure near fine features, because the jet drives coating into places masking cannot protect.
Coating a board that is not fully dry :: Dry first, since trapped moisture blisters the film and corrodes under it.
Assuming cure from the schedule alone :: Verify cure by the supplier's method, because shadowed or cool areas stay undercured.

## Glossary
Wet film thickness :: The coating thickness measured immediately after application, before solvent loss.
Volume solids :: The fraction of the wet coating that remains as dry film after cure.
Keep-out :: An area that must stay uncoated, defined in the coating program or mask layout.
FTIR conversion :: A spectroscopic check of how completely the coating has cured.
