## Thermal mass controls the board's response
Board thermal mass is the combined heat capacity and heat-transfer resistance of the laminate, copper planes, plated holes, components and fixtures. A thick board with heavy copper reaches oven temperature more slowly than a thin board with sparse copper, so one recipe yields different joint temperatures.

Measure at the pad or component body rather than the oven air, and check the result against the paste and component datasheets.

## Heavy planes act as heat sinks at the joint
Large power and ground planes pull heat away from small pads and can hold a joint below liquidus while the joints around it look normal. The effect peaks when a pad ties directly to a plane with little thermal relief, when the plane sits on an inner layer with short vias, or when the component carries high thermal mass.

Balance the heat flow through pad geometry, paste volume, preheat and airflow instead of simply raising peak temperature.

## Thermal relief is a heat-balance tool
Thermal relief spokes cut the copper area feeding heat away from a pad, which makes the pad easier to lift above liquidus. The relief still has to deliver electrical and mechanical integrity, so spoke count, width and clearance follow the board design rules and the stencil design.

On heavy planes, confirm that the pad reaches liquidus with the chosen paste and component. If it does not, adjust the profile or the layout before raising peak temperature.

## Mixed populations need a compromise profile
A board with large connectors, QFNs, BGAs and small chips holds joints that want different heat input. Large components and heavy planes need enough soak and time above liquidus, while small parts suffer from excessive peak or a long soak.

Build the profile around the most thermally demanding joint and the lowest component temperature limit, then confirm that every part stays inside its own specified limits.

## Dense and sparse layouts heat differently
Dense boards with many components and copper areas heat more uniformly and often need more total energy to reach liquidus. Sparse boards heat quickly and can develop local hot spots near small components or exposed copper.

When one recipe covers different panel designs, compare pad-level profiles, because oven air temperature hides the differences between dense and sparse areas.

## Alloy liquidus and standard limits
For lead-free paste, take the liquidus from the paste datasheet, then set time above liquidus and peak against the applicable standard and the component limits.

The average ramp of 1-3 °C/s comes from IPC-7530 and the paste datasheet. The 60-150 s time-above-liquidus window is J-STD-020 tL, which IPC-7530 typically narrows to 30-90 s. J-STD-020 supplies the classification temperatures that bound the peak: 260 °C small, 250 °C large and 245 °C very large at 1.6-2.5 mm thickness, where the full table is thickness times volume, every volume is 260 °C below 1.6 mm, and above 2.5 mm the limits fall to 250 °C small and 245 °C large and very large. Each carries tp, the time within 5 °C of that temperature, limited to 30 s. Verify the package size, the component datasheet and the paste supplier's recommendation.

## Move heat, not just temperature
When heavy planes hold pad temperature down, lengthen the soak or slow the conveyor to raise the board temperature before liquidus, then confirm that peak and time above liquidus stay inside their limits. Where sparse areas overheat, lower peak or reflow-zone airflow and let preheat bring the whole board up together.

Document thermocouple location, board thickness, copper weight, alloy, and the measured ramp, time above liquidus and peak, so the result can be repeated.

## Key takeaways
Profile the hardest joint, because the coolest pad under a heavy plane decides whether the assembly is sound.
Balance heat flow with soak, conveyor speed and thermal relief before raising peak, since a higher peak damages the sparse areas first.
Record thickness and copper weight with the profile, because those numbers explain why the same recipe behaves differently on the next board.

## Common mistakes
Raising peak temperature to wet one plane-tied pad :: Lengthen the soak or slow the conveyor, and check the sparse areas for overexposure.
Adding thermal relief after the profile is fixed :: Design the relief with the electrical and mechanical rules, then requalify the profile.
Copying a recipe between boards of different thickness :: Measure the new board, because thermal mass changes the whole curve.

## Glossary
Thermal mass :: The heat capacity of the board and its parts, which sets how fast they heat and cool.
Thermal relief :: Spokes in a plane connection that reduce heat flow into or out of a pad.
Heat sink :: A large copper area that conducts heat away from a joint.
Pad-level profile :: A temperature record taken at the joint rather than at the oven air.
