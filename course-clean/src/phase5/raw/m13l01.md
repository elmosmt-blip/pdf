## Test level starts with defect modes and access
A test strategy is a coverage decision, not a machine preference.

The main defect modes are opens, shorts, wrong or missing components, wrong polarity, solder defects, damaged traces and assembly errors that affect function. The chosen level catches defects near their point of occurrence, gives repairable diagnostics and fits the production volume.

Access, netlist quality and component mix usually decide whether a bed-of-nails fixture, probe access, scan chains or functional stimulus is practical.

## In-circuit test catches structural faults
In-circuit test drives a bed-of-nails fixture onto the test points and measures electrical structure in ohm, farad and volt.

It finds opens, shorts, wrong passive values, missing components and some polarity errors, which makes it strong on high-volume assemblies with good test-point access and a stable netlist.

It misses faults hidden under devices and in nets without access, and it is disturbed by guard circuits, power paths and component tolerance. Fixture cost and cycle time go into the choice as well, checked against the equipment datasheet.

## Flying probe is flexible structural test
Flying probe moves its probes to pads, vias or test points without a dedicated fixture.

That makes it the practical option for prototypes, low-volume builds and boards where fixture cost cannot be justified. It finds opens, shorts and some component errors, at the cost of longer cycle time and access limited by probe reach, pad size and obstruction.

It is usually paired with functional test when the structural coverage it delivers is incomplete.

## Boundary scan tests scan-capable interconnects
Boundary scan uses JTAG cells inside compliant devices to drive and observe pins while the board is powered.

It detects opens, shorts and some solder defects between scan-capable devices, including fine-pitch and ball-grid-array connections that are hard to probe. It does not measure passive values or power delivery, and it cannot see non-scan devices unless they are observable through scan logic.

Coverage depends on device support, scan chain integrity, and whether the netlist carries the relevant pins.

## Functional test proves behavior under intended conditions
Functional test applies inputs and checks outputs, timing in second, power in volt and ampere, communication behaviour and thermal behaviour in degree Celsius.

It catches the assembly errors that stop the board working: wrong firmware, connector faults, power problems and some component mismatches.

It misses latent defects, marginal joints and wrong values that do not change behaviour under the test conditions. It is a good final screen, though diagnosis gets harder when the fault is intermittent or outside the exercised path.

## Coverage tradeoffs between structural and functional levels
Structural tests locate a defect at the point where it occurs, and functional tests prove that the system behaves.

A structural test can pass a board that fails functionally, because it does not exercise dynamic loading, timing or analogue performance. A functional test can pass a board with a latent defect, because the fault is never excited.

A practical strategy combines structural coverage for manufacturing defects with functional coverage for operational requirements, and states which defect classes each level owns.

## Choosing a strategy with reasons
Rank defect risk, access, volume and repair data, then choose.

High-volume dense assemblies with good test points usually take in-circuit test plus functional test. Low-volume and prototype builds take flying probe plus functional, which avoids fixture cost. Dense digital assemblies with strong JTAG support take boundary scan plus functional to cover the hard-to-probe nets.

Mixed-signal and power assemblies need power-path checks in the structural level and load plus thermal conditions in the functional level. Check coverage against the probe and equipment datasheet before committing the line.

## Key takeaways
Use structural test to locate and functional test to prove, because each answers a question the other cannot.
Read a coverage number with its access limit, since a net without a test point has no in-circuit coverage however dense the fixture.
Add functional load and temperature where the product will see them, because nominal conditions hide marginal joints.

## Common mistakes
Claiming full structural coverage from a fixture with missing test points :: Subtract the inaccessible nets, because a bed of nails only touches what it can reach.
Treating a functional pass as proof of a sound joint :: Add boundary scan or X-ray on hidden joints, since a marginal connection can conduct under no load.
Buying a fixture for a prototype run :: Use flying probe, because fixture cost needs volume to spread over.

## Glossary
Bed-of-nails :: A fixture with spring-loaded probes that contact all test points at once.
Guard circuit :: A driven node that isolates a component so its value can be measured in circuit.
Boundary scan :: A test architecture that drives and observes device pins through internal scan cells.
Latent defect :: A fault that is present but does not show under the test conditions applied.
