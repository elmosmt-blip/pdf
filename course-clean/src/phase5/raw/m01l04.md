## Why incoming component verification exists
Incoming verification is the first engineering control that keeps wrong, damaged, expired, mixed or suspect material out of production. Before release, the receiving engineer has to prove four things for components, paste and bare boards alike: the item is the correct part, it traces to a manufacturer or supplier lot, it was handled inside its storage and process limits, and it meets the contractual quality and compliance requirements.

The output is a record, not a stamp. Purchasing, quality, engineering and the supplier all read it, so it names the purchase order, part number, lot or date code, quantity, documents reviewed, physical findings, disposition and any supplier action.

## Documents for components
A component lot normally arrives with a packing list, a delivery note or invoice, a certificate of conformance, manufacturer lot traceability and the special handling labels the part needs. The packing list fixes what was shipped and how much; the certificate of conformance states conformity to the purchase order and specification without replacing physical verification; lot traceability ties reels, trays, tubes or waffle packs to one manufacturer lot, which is what recall, failure analysis and counterfeit investigation depend on.

Moisture-sensitive devices come in a moisture barrier bag with desiccant, a humidity indicator card and an MSL label consistent with IPC/JEDEC J-STD-033. Where the contract calls for them, declarations such as RoHS or REACH prove the supplier's declared compliance status, not the composition of every piece in the lot.

## Documents for solder paste
Paste is a process material, so its record has to prove chemistry, condition and usable life. The certificate of analysis or conformance identifies alloy, powder type, flux chemistry, metal content, lot number, manufacture date, expiration date, storage temperature and any mixing or warming instruction. That makes it evidence of the specified formulation and of shelf life, nothing more.

A safety data sheet, shipped or available through the supplier's controlled portal, covers hazard classification and handling requirements rather than process quality. Check the lot number on the jar or cartridge against the certificate, inspect the seal, and confirm the paste never left the conditions in its datasheet. A missing certificate holds the lot in quarantine until traceable documentation arrives.

## Documents for bare PCBs
Bare boards need documentation that proves material, construction and acceptability: a packing list, a certificate of conformance and, where the contract requires it, qualification or performance evidence against IPC-6012. The certificate identifies part number, revision, layer count, material, copper weight, finish, panel dimensions, lot number and any special requirement such as controlled impedance, plating thickness or flammability.

Material declarations may reference IPC-4101, and flammability may reference UL 94. Visual and dimensional features are commonly judged against IPC-A-600, though the purchase order can impose tighter limits. Before release, match revision, material, finish and panel size to the drawing and the purchase order.

## Verify part number, package and date code
Physical verification runs outside in: carton, then inner packaging, then component marking. Supplier label, purchase order and certificate must agree on part number, manufacturer, package type, quantity and lot or date code. On tape and reel, check tape width, cover tape, reel size and orientation against the placement equipment and the datasheet; on trays, tubes and waffle packs, check for mixed cavities and cracked carriers.

Read date codes against the manufacturer's own convention, and ask when the convention is unknown rather than guessing. Altered, laser-etched, re-marked or inconsistent marking makes the lot suspect, so quarantine it. Where counterfeit risk is high, add X-ray, decapsulation, electrical test or authorised-distributor traceability.

## Counterfeit red flags
Counterfeit avoidance is an incoming engineering control, not only a purchasing question. Red flags include an unusually low price, an unusually short lead time, missing manufacturer traceability, an unknown or unauthorised distributor, mixed date codes, mixed manufacturers, altered labels, bulk repackaging, old date codes, inconsistent tape or reel packaging and markings unlike the manufacturer's normal style.

None of these proves fraud, but any of them justifies quarantine and further verification. For high-risk parts, buy from authorised distributors, demand manufacturer traceability and apply controls consistent with SAE AS6081. Isolate a suspected lot, photograph the packaging and markings, record lot and date codes and ask the supplier for evidence; if traceability never arrives or verification fails, reject the lot and update the approved supplier list or risk assessment.

## Accept, quarantine, reject
Acceptance needs complete documentation, correct identity, acceptable condition and no unresolved red flag. Quarantine covers the lot that may be usable but whose evidence is incomplete or suspect: missing traceability, mixed date codes, damaged moisture barrier packaging, or an age that needs engineering review. Reject applies to the wrong, expired or damaged lot, to one that fails verification, and to one whose authenticity cannot be proven.

Disposition follows the quality management system — ISO 9001, or ANSI/AS9100 where the contract invokes it — which requires nonconforming output to be controlled and documented. A record the supplier can act on carries the purchase order, part number, lot or date code, quantities received, accepted, quarantined and rejected, the objective evidence, required containment, a corrective action request and a response due date. Say what was observed and where, not that the packaging was bad.

## Key takeaways
Prove identity, traceability, handling and compliance before release, and write the result as a record the supplier and the line can both act on.
Check lot traceability and date-code consistency as carefully as the certificate, because repackaged or mixed stock is what counterfeiting looks like at receiving.
Use accept, quarantine and reject deliberately: incomplete evidence is a quarantine, not an acceptance with a note.

## Common mistakes
Accepting a lot on a certificate of conformance alone :: Verify the physical marking, the lot code and the inner labels against the certificate before release.
Reading date codes with an assumed convention :: Confirm the manufacturer's date-code format, then compute the age against the purchase-order limit.
Recording a vague finding such as bad packaging :: State what was observed, where, on which lot, and what containment and corrective action you require.

## Glossary
Manufacturer lot traceability :: Evidence linking the packaged parts to a specific manufacturing lot at the original manufacturer.
Counterfeit part :: A part whose identity, grade or origin is misrepresented, including re-marked, refurbished or unauthorised copies.
Quarantine :: The controlled holding of material whose disposition is not yet established.
Date code :: The manufacturer's code for the production date of a lot, read against that manufacturer's convention.
