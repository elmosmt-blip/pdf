## What shadowing means at the wave
Shadowing is a wetting or fill defect caused when a component, pallet, fixture or neighbouring part blocks the solder wave, flux or preheat from reaching a pad, lead or joint.

At the wave it is more than optical obstruction: local cooling, flux depletion, turbulence and poor drainage all contribute. Predict it by asking two questions — can solder reach the joint from the wave side, and can the joint drain after contact?

## Layout and orientation as the first predictor
Layout predicts shadowing when a tall or wide component sits upstream of a smaller joint in the wave direction. A connector, shield, transformer or tall electrolytic capacitor leaves a low-energy zone behind it.

Draw the wave direction on the assembly drawing and mark the solder-side access path. A downstream joint behind a body, pallet wall or neighbouring part will show incomplete fill, voiding or cold joints. Confirm on a test coupon, because turbulence can stretch or shorten the affected zone.

## Board and wave direction
Board direction changes the shadow length, because the same component presents a different projected height to the wave.

A long body parallel to the wave direction can shade a whole row of joints, while rotating the panel or the component 90 degrees cuts the projected length. Check conveyor direction, wave angle and board angle together. Where the line allows panel rotation, compare the shadowed side before and after; where it does not, use pallet clearance or masking to compensate.

## Pallets, fixtures and masking
Pallets shadow when holes are too small, walls too thick, or support rails cover pads. They also trap flux, shield preheat and hold heat that changes drainage.

A good pallet opens the solder side around each joint, leaves clearance around leads, and avoids covering adjacent pads. Masking tape, kapton or pallet masking removes shadowing by blocking solder from areas that should stay dry, and the mask edge must not bridge into the joint. Check the pallet material, adhesive and wave equipment datasheet.

## Lead geometry and pad access
Lead geometry controls how much solder enters the hole and wets the pad. Long leads, tight lead-to-hole clearance, bent leads and large bodies all reduce capillary action and drainage.

Axial and radial parts behave differently: an axial lead can be shaded by a body sitting close to the board, while a radial lead can be shaded by adjacent leads or a pallet wall. Check lead diameter, hole diameter, pad size and seating height against your drill and plating data.

## Sequence changes that reduce shadowing
Sequence can change at layout, pallet or line level. Place shadow-prone joints where the wave has stabilised, keep them out of the wake of tall parts, and use a pallet that exposes the joint from the wave side.

On some lines a second wave, a different wave angle or a selective wave improves access. Where a downstream part blocks drainage, move the part, rotate the panel or add a pallet opening. Confirm the change with cross-section or wetting inspection, not only with appearance.

## A practical prediction method
Start with a simple line-of-sight model: project the component height along the wave angle to estimate the shadow zone, then add a margin for turbulence and flux depletion.

Treat that model as a screening tool and not a guarantee. For production, run a coupon in the suspected orientation, inspect the joint, and adjust direction, masking or sequence. Check against your paste, wave and pallet datasheets, because the same layout behaves differently on other conveyors and wave types.

## Key takeaways
Check whether solder can reach the joint and whether it can drain, because both halves of the shadow problem need fixing.
Change the projected height — by rotating board or component — before adding fixtures, since geometry is the cheapest lever.
Screen with a line-of-sight model and confirm on a coupon, because turbulence decides the real edge of the shadow.

## Common mistakes
Judging shadowing from the top-side appearance :: Inspect the solder side and cross-section, since the top looks the same either way.
Adding masking without checking the mask edge :: Keep the edge clear of the joint, or the mask itself bridges the gap.
Trusting the geometric shadow alone :: Add a turbulence margin and confirm on a coupon.

## Glossary
Low-energy zone :: The region behind an obstacle where the wave arrives weakened and cooled.
Projected height :: The obstacle dimension that faces the wave travel direction.
Pallet opening :: The cut-out that exposes a joint to the wave through the fixture.
Drainage :: How readily solder leaves the joint after the wave passes.
