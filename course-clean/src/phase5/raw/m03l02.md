## Area ratio: what it measures
Area ratio is the aperture opening area divided by the sidewall area of the stencil aperture. On a rectangular aperture the opening is width times length, and the sidewall is the perimeter times the foil thickness.

IPC-7525C treats area ratio as a key indicator of paste release, because the paste has to separate from those walls and transfer to the pad.

## Area ratio and paste release
A higher area ratio gives the paste more opening area against the wall it must leave, and transfer tends to improve with it. A low ratio means the wall contact area is large next to the opening, so paste sticks in the aperture and the pad gets an inadequate deposit.

IPC-7525C gives area-ratio guidance for stencil design, but the practical release limit also depends on paste rheology, wall finish, print speed, snap-off distance and squeegee pressure. Check all of it against your paste and equipment datasheet.

## Aspect ratio: what it measures
Aspect ratio is the aperture dimension divided by foil thickness. On a rectangular aperture you use the smallest dimension, because it controls the narrowest paste channel. IPC-7525C discusses aspect ratio as a stencil geometry parameter tied to paste release and aperture wall effects.

## Aspect ratio and fine-pitch printing
As pitch falls, apertures shrink while foil thickness often stays where it was, so aspect ratio rises and area ratio drops. High aspect ratio apertures make release more sensitive to print parameters and wall condition.

On fine-pitch designs, review aspect ratio together with area ratio, paste type and SPI results. A single pass or fail number cannot carry a fine-pitch decision.

## Deposit volume: theoretical and expected
The theoretical maximum deposit is aperture area multiplied by foil thickness. The expected deposit is lower, because paste never transfers perfectly, and it is commonly estimated by multiplying the theoretical volume by a transfer efficiency factor.

Take that factor from your paste, stencil, printer and SPI data. A generic assumption hides the process you are actually running.

## Fine-pitch consequences
Fine-pitch apertures can produce low area ratio, high aspect ratio and small deposit volume at the same time. The joint then risks insufficient paste, voids, opens or inconsistent transfer, and the process becomes sensitive to paste slump, stencil wear, wall roughness and print parameter drift.

## The practical release decision
Combine area ratio, aspect ratio, expected deposit volume and process data. Where area ratio sits near or below the IPC-7525C guidance range, or the expected volume is close to the minimum the component and pad need, review the design.

The review covers foil thickness, aperture size within the pad, wall finish, paste selection and print parameters.

## Key takeaways
Calculate area ratio and aspect ratio from the same three numbers — aperture dimensions and foil thickness — before the stencil is ordered.
Compare the expected deposit with the joint requirement, because a good area ratio can still leave too little paste.
Treat a low area ratio as a design review trigger supported by SPI data, not as an automatic rejection.

## Common mistakes
Increasing foil thickness to raise paste volume :: Thicker foil lowers area ratio and costs release; raise aperture area or use a step stencil instead.
Judging a fine-pitch aperture on area ratio alone :: Read aspect ratio and the SPI deposit data with it, since both drive transfer.
Using a textbook transfer efficiency in the design review :: Take the factor from your own printer, paste and SPI results.

## Glossary
Area ratio :: Aperture opening area divided by sidewall area, the standard indicator of paste release in stencil design.
Aspect ratio :: Aperture width divided by foil thickness on the narrowest aperture dimension.
Transfer efficiency :: The fraction of theoretical aperture volume that reaches the pad as a deposit.
Fine pitch :: Component lead spacing small enough that aperture dimensions approach the limits of paste release.
