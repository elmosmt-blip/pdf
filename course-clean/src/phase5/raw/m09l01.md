## The process window
A wave soldering window is the interaction of flux application, preheat, wave contact and cooling.

On lead-free alloy the window narrows, because wetting and intermetallic formation both need adequate board temperature and short contact. Set the window by thermocouple profiling and cross-section, then verify against your paste and equipment datasheet.

## Preheat for lead-free wave
Preheat activates flux, drives off solvents and brings board and leads to a temperature where solder wets without thermal shock.

Establish the top-side temperature before the wave by profiling. Common practice uses a soak plateau, and the exact value depends on board mass, flux and equipment. Place thermocouples on heavy ground planes and on small pads, and follow IPC-7530 for the profiling method.

## Contact time and conveyor speed
Wave length and conveyor speed set contact time. Too short gives insufficient fill and cold joints; too long brings excess solder, bridging and thermal stress.

Adjust conveyor speed together with wave height and pump condition, and verify the result with cross-sections and solder weight rather than with the display.

## Wave height and nozzle condition
Wave height controls wetting pressure and contact. A wave too high floods through-holes and makes icicles; one too low misses pads.

Nozzle clogging and pump wear change the effective contact time even when the speed has not moved, which is why wave height belongs in the same check as speed.

## Nitrogen
Nitrogen reduces oxidation of solder and flux residue, improves wetting, lowers dross and can cut bridging. It does not replace correct preheat or flux selection.

Control and monitor the oxygen level. The benefit is largest on fine-pitch and high-density boards, where the same wetting margin buys more.

## Flux management
Flux solids, activation and application density decide residue, wetting and cleaning. Too little flux gives non-wetting and shading; too much leaves residue, bridges and a cleaning burden.

Measure and trend flux density, and evaluate residue by cross-section and ion chromatography or an equivalent method.

## Cross-section diagnosis
Cross-sections show whether the window is right. Shading points to oxidation or insufficient flux activity. Excess solder points to too high a wave, too long contact or poor drainage. Insufficient fill points to low preheat, short contact, blocked holes or poor wetting.

Judge the result against the IPC-A-610 acceptance criteria for the product class.

## Key takeaways
Set conveyor speed from the wave contact length and the required contact time, then check it against cross-sections rather than the display.
Profile the top-side temperature on heavy planes and small pads, because preheat is what decides wetting without thermal shock.
Treat nitrogen as a wetting aid, not as a substitute for preheat and flux selection.

## Common mistakes
Lowering conveyor speed to fix insufficient fill without checking preheat :: Profile the board first, since cold holes cannot be filled by dwelling longer.
Judging the wave by its height on the pump only :: Look at the wetting pressure across the board, because nozzle clogging changes it locally.
Adding nitrogen to compensate for a weak flux :: Fix the flux selection and density, then measure the oxygen benefit separately.

## Glossary
Contact time :: The interval a board spends in contact with the solder wave.
Shading :: Incomplete wetting on a through-hole or pad caused by oxidation or insufficient flux activity.
Dross :: Oxide and intermetallic debris that forms on molten solder.
Ion chromatography :: A test that measures ionic contamination left by flux residues.
