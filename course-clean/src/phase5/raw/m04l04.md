## What SPI measures and how to read it
SPI compares the printed deposit with the stencil aperture volume. Volume percent is usually measured deposit volume divided by nominal aperture volume, so 100 % means the aperture filled to stencil thickness and released without loss.

Height is the mean or maximum deposit thickness, area is the footprint of paste on the board, and offset is the centre-to-centre displacement between aperture and deposit. Histograms show the spread around the mean: a narrow one means repeatable release, a bimodal one usually means two release states, clean stencil against partially blocked apertures. Trends expose paste ageing, stencil fouling, squeegee wear and temperature drift. Judge the print against the IPC acceptance criteria for the product class.

## Viscosity, thixotropy and metal load
Viscosity controls how much shear it takes to push paste through the aperture and how much it resists flowing afterwards. Thixotropy is the recovery of viscosity after shear: adequate recovery holds the deposit's shape, slow recovery lets it slump.

Metal load, the powder fraction, drives density, volume and rheology. A higher metal load can raise deposit height for the same stencil thickness, and it may also raise viscosity and hurt wetting when the flux balance does not match. Read the paste datasheet for metal load, viscosity at the relevant shear rates and recommended stencil thickness.

## Slump, bleed and bridging
Slump is lateral flow after release, seen as more area and less height. It comes from low viscosity, excessive paste temperature, too much squeegee shear or a slow snap-off.

Bleed is paste sitting on the board between apertures, usually from underside contamination, insufficient wiping or paste that is too fluid. Bridging joins adjacent apertures and is likelier when paste is too stiff, the underside is dirty, or separation is too high. Read area and height together: high area with low height suggests slump, high area with normal height suggests bleed or offset.

## Handling and paste life on the stencil
Paste life is how long the paste stays printable after it reaches the stencil. Solvent evaporation, temperature rise and repeated rolling all raise viscosity and cut slump resistance.

Paste that prints well at the start of a shift can print low volume later if the underside dries or the paste stiffens. Keep the stencil clean, control paste temperature and check open time against the datasheet. When the SPI trend shows gradual volume loss, look at paste handling before touching the squeegee settings.

## Squeegee, snap-off, separation and wipe
Pressure and speed control shear and fill. More pressure improves fill and can smear paste or damage the stencil; less speed improves fill and costs throughput.

Snap-off speed decides how fast the board leaves the stencil: too fast pulls paste and drops height, too slow smears. Separation is the distance the board moves before snap-off: too much stretches the paste and lowers height, too little smears. Wipe length and pressure clean the underside; too little leaves paste that bleeds, too much strips apertures or dries the stencil edge.

Typical starting points are stencil thickness 0.10-0.15 mm, squeegee speed 25-40 mm/s, squeegee pressure 5-15 N, separation 0.5-1.5 mm and wipe length 1-3 mm, all verified against your paste and equipment datasheet.

## Reading SPI for the dominant defect mode
Low volume percent with low height and normal area usually means incomplete fill or poor release. Low volume percent with high area suggests slump or bleed. Normal volume percent with high offset means alignment or registration. High volume percent with high area and low height can mean excessive wipe pressure or paste that is too fluid.

Histograms separate random variation from drift: a broad one points to unstable snap-off or paste rheology, a shifted mean points to a setting or the paste condition. Typical handling is 20-30 °C and 40-60 % RH with open time often 4-8 h, verified against the paste and equipment datasheet.

## Tuning direction for a stated defect
Low volume and low height with a clean SPI area call for slightly more squeegee pressure, less squeegee speed, slower snap-off, less separation, and less wipe pressure or length if either is excessive.

Low volume with high area calls for less squeegee pressure, less speed, faster snap-off and less separation. Bleed at normal volume calls for a longer or harder wipe and a clean stencil. Bridging calls for less separation, a more effective wipe and a viscosity check. Change one setting at a time and verify against the datasheet.

## Key takeaways
Read volume percent with height and area, because the same volume percentage means different defects at different heights.
Treat a broad histogram and a drifting mean as two different messages: instability against a real change.
Check paste age, temperature and open time before changing printer settings when volume falls slowly over a shift.

## Common mistakes
Chasing a falling volume trend with squeegee pressure :: Check paste temperature, open time and stencil cleanliness first.
Reading volume percent alone :: Confirm height and area, since slump and incomplete fill both lower volume but need opposite responses.
Changing several settings in one trial :: Change one variable per trial, or the result tells you nothing about which setting mattered.

## Glossary
Volume percent :: Measured deposit volume divided by nominal aperture volume, expressed as a percentage.
Thixotropy :: The recovery of paste viscosity after shear, which lets a printed deposit hold its shape.
Slump :: Lateral flow of the deposit after release, showing as larger area and lower height.
Metal load :: The percentage of metal powder by weight in the solder paste.
