## What SPI evidence means on the print stage
Solder paste inspection is process evidence for the print step, not final joint acceptance. It reports what the stencil deposited before reflow: volume, height, area, offset, histogram shape and trend over time.

Use it to confirm the print process is stable and centred, and to name the dominant defect mode before boards move downstream. A good SPI result does not guarantee good soldering; a poor or drifting one is a strong early signal that the print step needs attention.

## Reading volume and height together
Volume is the most useful single metric because it combines area and height. Low volume with normal height points to reduced paste area: incomplete aperture fill, partial clogging, a weak squeegee roll, or paste that does not release cleanly.

Low volume with normal area points to reduced height: collapse, excessive wipe, low viscosity, or insufficient transfer. High volume at normal height points to area expansion, bleed or underside contamination.

## Reading area and offset
Area says whether the footprint matches the aperture. Low area points to incomplete fill, paste dry-out, clogging or poor snap-off. High area points to bleed, underside paste, excessive pressure, or a separation fast enough to smear the deposit.

Offset is positional error. A consistent direction suggests alignment, vision, conveyor, stencil tension or board support. A random one suggests board handling, fiducial quality, camera focus or unstable seating.

## Reading histograms
A histogram counts how many prints fall at each value. A narrow peak near target is what you want.

A wide distribution means an inconsistent process, often from unstable paste temperature, cleaning variation, squeegee wear or board-to-board support differences. A low-skewed histogram suggests paste depletion, aperture clogging, insufficient pressure or paste too viscous. A high-skewed one suggests overfill, bleed or underside paste. A bimodal shape means two populations are being mixed: two stencil zones, two board types, two squeegee passes or two SPI calibration regions.

## Reading the trend
Trend charts beat isolated measurements. A step change means something changed suddenly: paste lot, stencil, squeegee blade, tension adjustment, SPI calibration or a maintenance action. A gradual drift means something is changing slowly: viscosity, ambient temperature, stencil wear, cleaning effectiveness or blade wear.

Establish whether the trend is stable, drifting or stepped before adjusting anything, because adjusting an unstable process hides the real cause.

## Capability for volume and height
Capability compares the process spread with the specification limits. Cp describes spread alone — whether the process could fit inside the limits if centred. Cpk adds centring — whether it actually is centred enough to meet them.

For paste printing, use the control plan limits for the product and paste system, not only the SPI machine tolerance. Capability means something only while the process is stable: with a step, a drift or repeated out-of-limit points, correct the process first, then recalculate.

## Stop or adjust
A stable, centred, capable process against the control plan continues and gets monitored. A stable but off-centre process is adjusted through the levers that move transfer: paste temperature, squeegee pressure, squeegee speed, snap-off speed, cleaning frequency and underside wipe.

An unstable process stops for investigation before any small parameter change. Repeated out-of-limit prints, a systematic offset, or capability that will not come back after a controlled adjustment stops the line, quarantines suspect boards, and gets verified against SPI maps, stencil inspection and cross-section or pull-back evidence.

## Key takeaways
Judge capability only on a stable process, because Cp and Cpk on a drifting trend describe a process that no longer exists.
Read volume with height and area before naming a defect, since volume alone confuses under-fill with spreading.
Distinguish a step change from a gradual drift, because the first points to an event and the second to wear.

## Common mistakes
Recalculating capability after every process adjustment :: Establish stability on a control chart first, then compute capability from that data.
Adjusting print parameters during a drift before checking paste age :: Check paste temperature, open time and stencil cleanliness first, then touch the machine.
Reading a bimodal histogram as random variation :: Find the two populations — panel side, stencil zone, board type — before changing limits.

## Glossary
Cp :: The specification width divided by six standard deviations, a measure of spread alone.
Cpk :: The distance from the mean to the nearer specification limit in standard deviations, a measure of spread and centring.
Bimodal histogram :: A distribution with two peaks, showing that two different populations are being measured together.
Step change :: An abrupt shift in a trend chart caused by a single event in the process.
