## Why boundary scan matters for BGA assemblies
Boundary scan earns its place on BGA assemblies because the package hides the solder joints and the escape routing, so a tester cannot reach every net with a probe.

IEEE Std 1149.1 (JTAG) defines a serial test access port and boundary-scan cells that let a tester drive or observe device pins through a handful of control lines.

At 0.5 mm pitch the value is not in replacing every other method but in giving electrical access to digital nets that would otherwise have none.

## Boundary-scan structure
A scan-capable device contains a TAP controller, an instruction register and data registers, and the boundary-scan cells sit between the internal core and the package pins.

In EXTEST the tester shifts a pattern into the chain, updates the cells and captures the pin states. TCK clocks the state machine, TMS controls the transitions, TDI shifts data in, TDO shifts data out, and TRST is optional.

Chains can be daisy-chained across devices, which is convenient until chain length and TCK frequency start to cost test time.

## What boundary scan can find
Boundary scan finds digital interconnect defects between scan cells: opens, shorts, stuck-at faults and some bridging where both ends of the net are scan-capable.

It reads IDCODE to confirm device presence and orientation, and SAMPLE/PRELOAD observes normal operation. That makes it useful in-system and on boards where probe access is poor.

Coverage depends on the accuracy of the BSDL file and the netlist, and on whether the pins in question are actually configured as scan cells.

## What boundary scan cannot find
Boundary scan cannot find a defect that leaves the scan cell's logic state unchanged, or one outside the chain.

Non-scan passives, analogue circuits, power distribution, clocks, reset, configuration pins and high-speed interfaces stay invisible unless extra test structures exist. It says nothing about solder joint quality, mechanical strength, contamination, thermal behaviour or parametric performance such as rise time, noise margin or current draw.

A short on a net with one non-scan end waits for another method to catch it. The scan port itself is defined by IEEE Std 1149.1, and nothing outside that definition is visible to it.

## Compare ICT, flying probe, boundary scan and functional test
Compare by access, coverage, speed and cost.

In-circuit test uses a bed of nails and can check many passive and pin-level faults quickly, at the cost of test points and fixture design, and fine-pitch BGA escapes may still be out of reach. Flying probe avoids the fixture and suits prototypes, and it is slower and still needs probe access.

Boundary scan needs few access points and works well on BGA digital nets, given scan-capable devices, BSDL files and design discipline. Functional test proves the system and is the slowest to diagnose.

## Choosing a test strategy
Choose from the assembly mix.

A high-volume BGA board with many scan-capable digital devices takes boundary scan for digital interconnect, functional test for system behaviour, and in-circuit or flying probe for non-scan passives where test points exist.

A prototype of a few boards gets faster feedback from flying probe plus boundary scan plus functional test than from a full fixture. On a mixed-signal board boundary scan may cover a minority of nets, so functional test carries more of the weight.

## Design-for-test requests
Specify DFT as design requests with a rationale.

Ask for boundary-scan support on all digital devices with BSDL files, IDCODE values and a netlist so the tester can build chains. Ask for a stable JTAG header or test points, with TCK, TMS, TDI, TDO and optional TRST routed per IEEE Std 1149.1 (JTAG).

Ask for test points on non-scan passives, power, ground, clocks, reset and configuration pins, and for chains short enough for the cycle time the tester needs.

## Practical limits and verification
Chain length, TCK frequency, power state and pin multiplexing are the practical limits.

Some devices need power and configuration before scan access, others support limited unpowered modes, so check the device datasheet. Long chains raise test time and can degrade signal integrity; short, balanced groups help.

Keep JTAG traces away from high-speed clocks and sensitive analogue nets, and verify signal quality by simulation or measurement before the design freezes.

## Key takeaways
Read boundary scan as coverage of digital nets, because everything outside the chain is untested however clean the report looks.
Keep chains short and balanced, since chain length spends cycle time on every vector.
Ask for scan support and BSDL files at design review, because a device chosen without them has no coverage to add later.

## Common mistakes
Treating a passing scan report as full board coverage :: Publish the untested net list, because reset, clock and power usually sit outside the chain.
Designing long daisy chains for layout convenience :: Split into short chains, because every bit shifts on every vector.
Assuming scan access needs no power sequencing :: Check the device state requirement, since some devices need configuration before scan works.

## Glossary
BSDL :: The boundary-scan description language file that tells the tester how a device's scan cells are arranged.
EXTEST :: The scan instruction that drives and observes package pins for interconnect testing.
IDCODE :: A device identification register readable through the scan port.
TAP controller :: The state machine inside a scan-capable device that sequences test access port operations.
