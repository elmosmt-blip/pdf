## What a thermal survey measures
A thermal survey records the temperature the board, components and solder joints see as they pass through the oven. The zone setpoints are control targets, not proof that any joint reached temperature.

The measurable outcome is the temperature at the coolest and hottest locations on a representative coupon or production panel, compared with the paste liquidus and solidus, the time above liquidus and the component peak limit. Run the survey at the production panel orientation, conveyor speed and loading pattern; a bare coupon or a different layout measures a process you do not run.

The question the survey answers is whether the actual joint temperatures are inside the window, not whether the oven display matches the recipe.

## Thermocouple selection and attachment
Use a fine-gauge type K thermocouple with a calibrated logger. Attach the junction to the pad, trace or component body that represents the joint under evaluation.

High-temperature tape works if it is rated for the peak and does not lift in handling. A small solder blob improves thermal contact and changes the local thermal mass, so use one method consistently across surveys. Secure the wire so the junction cannot peel away — a lifted junction reads low because it sees air. A junction on a large copper area can read high or lag the joint.

Check tape, adhesive and logger ratings against your equipment datasheet.

## Test coupon design
A coupon reproduces the materials and thermal mass of the production board: same laminate, copper distribution, component type, paste deposit and panel position.

Where the product carries a large ground plane, a dense BGA, a heavy connector or mixed SMT and THT, include those features, or run the survey on a sacrificial production panel. A simple strip with small pads gives a clean profile and misses the coolest joint under the ground plane.

Place the coupon where production boards sit, because airflow and radiant heating vary across oven width and length. A coupon that is too light heats faster than the real board and returns a false pass.

## Finding the coolest and hottest sites
The coolest site is usually under a large ground plane, near a cold edge, under a heavy component, or at a panel location with poor airflow. The hottest may be near a heater, on a small low-mass component, or at an edge exposed to radiation.

Run several thermocouples at once and compare the curves. A wide spread between sites means the setpoints no longer describe the process. The coolest site decides whether the paste reaches liquidus and forms a joint; the hottest decides whether anything nearby is overexposed. One thermocouple on a fast pad hides a cold joint elsewhere.

## Reading the zones
Read the board temperature in each zone. Preheat brings the board up without thermal shock, soak narrows the spread and activates flux, reflow holds the joint above liquidus long enough to wet, and cooling solidifies without excessive stress.

For lead-free paste the liquidus comes from the paste datasheet, and the time above liquidus is checked against the paste and component datasheets. Component peak limits are commonly referenced to IPC/JEDEC J-STD-020, while the component datasheet and the applicable moisture sensitivity level set the final limit. Check every practice-based value against your paste and equipment datasheet.

## Measurement uncertainty and repeatability
A measured temperature carries thermocouple error, logger error, attachment lag and placement error. Use a logger with a calibration certificate and record the sampling interval.

Keep the attachment method identical between surveys, so a change in the profile comes from the oven. A junction lifted by tape reads low, one soldered to a large plane reads high or late, a loose wire gives intermittent contact, and a logger resting on a hot conveyor rail shifts its reference junction.

Document thermocouple type, logger model, calibration status, attachment method and the exact location of every junction. Without that record, two surveys can differ while the oven has not changed.

## Decision rule
Accept the survey when the coolest joint reaches liquidus with enough time above it and the hottest joint stays below the component and board limits.

A marginal coolest site calls for a profile change or an investigation of heat transfer, panel loading and airflow. A hottest site close to a limit calls for a lower peak or better cooling. A spread too wide between sites needs better uniformity rather than a higher setpoint.

Document the coupon, thermocouple locations, oven settings, measured minimum and maximum temperatures and any deviation. The result should state whether the joints are acceptable, not whether the recipe was followed.

## Key takeaways
Attach thermocouples to the joint or body under test, because a junction on a convenient pad measures a board you do not build.
Find the coolest and hottest sites together, since one thermocouple can pass a survey that fails at the ground plane.
Record the attachment method and location with every survey, or the next run cannot be compared with this one.

## Common mistakes
Surveying a bare coupon or test strip :: Use a coupon with the production copper and components, or a sacrificial production panel.
Reading the oven display as joint temperature :: Measure on the board, because setpoints say nothing about joint thermal lag.
Changing the profile before checking the attachment :: Confirm the junction is secure and correctly placed, then adjust the oven.

## Glossary
Thermal survey :: A recorded temperature history from thermocouples on a representative board.
Test coupon :: A sample board that reproduces the production laminate, copper and component mass.
Thermal lag :: The delay between oven air temperature and the temperature reached at the joint.
Worst-case site :: The location on the board that reaches the lowest or the highest temperature.
