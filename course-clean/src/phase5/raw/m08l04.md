## Shadowing is directional
At the wave the board underside passes over a stationary solder wave, and tall components, connectors, heat sinks, shielding cans and pallet supports deflect it, leaving pads downstream dry, underfilled or poorly wetted.

The preparation job is to find the shadow sources, locate the critical pads, and choose board direction, lead clinch and masking so no critical pad sits in the wake of a tall object. Judge wetting and flux residues against the IPC acceptance criteria for electronic assemblies.

## Choose board direction first
For a single board or panel, rotate the layout so the long axis of tall rectangular components runs perpendicular to the conveyor direction where possible.

Where critical pads cannot move, rotate the board 90 degrees or use a small angle to move the wake off them. Prove the direction on a trial board, because wave turbulence, nozzle design and flux activity change the shadow length.

## Clinch and lead formation
Where leads are formed or clinched on the wave side, keep bends from lying across downstream pads. Prefer bends perpendicular to the wave, or directed away from critical pads.

Keep wave-side protrusion short and controlled, commonly 0.5-1.5 mm, verified against your equipment and flux datasheet. Long or angled leads act as small dams and lengthen the shadow.

## Carriers and pallets
A carrier or pallet holds the board flat, prevents bowing, and lets flux and air escape. Support pins and pallet edges must not block the wave path or cover pads.

Keep clearance under the board, often 1-2 mm, so the wave reaches the underside evenly. Check flatness and support placement: a bowed board creates local shadowing even with the right orientation.

## Masking
Masking covers areas that should not be soldered and can cut shadowing from large metal parts. Use high-temperature tape or liquid mask that survives preheat and wave contact.

Cover connector bodies, heat sinks, test points and surface-mount devices not meant for the wave. Mask edges should be flat and sealed, and the mask must not trap flux or block drainage. Check the material against your flux and equipment datasheet.

## Taping
Tape secures masks and protects edges. Use high-temperature adhesive tape whose release does not lift in preheat, and seal the edges so flux cannot wick underneath.

Leave drainage holes and pallet vents clear. After a trial, inspect for lifted tape, trapped flux and residue on adjacent pads, then set tape width and overlap — commonly 6-12 mm — from what the line shows.

## Verification loop
Before production, run a trial board with the chosen orientation, clinch, pallet and mask, and inspect pads downstream of tall components for wetting, solder volume and residue.

If shadowing persists, change one variable: rotate the board, change clinch direction, add masking, or adjust pallet support. Record the accepted combination so operators can reproduce it.

## Key takeaways
Set orientation before anything else, because a rotated board removes shadows that no amount of flux can cure.
Keep wave-side lead protrusion short and directed away from critical pads, since a bent lead forms its own dam.
Prove the combination on a trial board and record it, so the next shift runs the setup that worked.

## Common mistakes
Taping over drainage holes to protect a part :: Leave drainage clear, or flux pools and leaves residue on neighbouring pads.
Choosing orientation by board outline rather than by critical pads :: Rotate until the pads in the wake are the ones that can tolerate it.
Changing direction, masking and pallet support together :: Change one variable per trial, or the accepted setup cannot be explained.

## Glossary
Wake :: The region downstream of an obstacle where the solder wave is disturbed or absent.
Wave-side lead protrusion :: The length of lead standing proud of the solder side after clinching.
Carrier :: A fixture that holds the board flat through the wave.
Drainage (flux and gas venting) :: The path that lets flux and air escape from under a board or mask.
