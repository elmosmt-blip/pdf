## Shadowing is a layout problem
Shadowing happens when a component, a neighbouring part or a fixture blocks molten solder from reaching a through-hole joint at the wave.

The wave sees the board as a sequence of obstacles: tall parts, dense pin fields, large ground planes and closely spaced components each create local dry zones. Choose board direction, clinch and masking so solder can reach the hole and wet the annular ring without being deflected or trapped.

## Board direction controls the approach
Pick board direction so the wave reaches the side of the component least likely to shield its own joint. On a rectangular part, the long axis usually runs parallel to the wave direction where the body would otherwise shade its leads. On a connector or tall part, the joint side should face the incoming wave where possible, or the board is angled to shorten the shadow.

The angle is process-dependent: conveyor angles are typically small, often around 3 to 7 degrees, verified against the wave solder equipment datasheet and trial boards.

## Clinch geometry affects seating and access
Clinch holds the component through insertion and transport, and it also sets the gap between body and board.

A tight clinch improves seating and reduces stand-off while closing the gap under the body and restricting solder flow. A loose clinch improves access and allows vibration to lift or misalign the part. Many through-hole components do best with a controlled clinch that keeps the body stable while leaving a small solder-access gap; check clinch force and final stand-off against the insertion machine and the component datasheet.

## Stand-off trades seating against access
Stand-off is the distance between body and board surface. Too little traps flux, prevents solder reaching the joint and makes cleaning hard. Too much demands more solder, exposes leads to mechanical stress and costs board density.

For many radial and axial components a practical range is around 0.5 to 1.5 mm, verified against body geometry, pin length, flux activity and the wave profile. Components with moulded bodies or heat-sensitive materials follow the manufacturer's seating or stand-off limits.

## Masking can reduce shadowing
Masking protects areas that must not be soldered, and a poor layout worsens shadowing by blocking the wave path or trapping flux.

A good layout exposes the annular ring and lead while avoiding tall mask walls directly in front of the joint. Place mask edges so solder flows around the joint rather than through a narrow channel. Dense layouts often do better with partial masking or local openings than with a continuous band. Verify mask thickness, edge clearance and solder resist compatibility against the wave process.

## Polarity errors are insertion defects
Polarity errors put a polarised component in the wrong direction: electrolytic capacitors, diodes, LEDs, keyed connectors, ICs with a pin 1 indicator.

Feeder setup, tray orientation, vision misalignment, manual error or a program that disagrees with the physical part all cause them. A polarity error can pass visual inspection when nobody checks the body marking, so the inspection method must confirm marking orientation, not just the presence of the part.

## Insertion damage is usually mechanical
Insertion damage covers bent leads, crushed bodies, cracked ceramics, damaged pin plating and torn lands.

Misaligned feeders, excessive insertion force, worn insertion heads, poor pin straightening and weak board support cause it. The location points to the cause: damage on one board side suggests a feeder or head offset, while damage confined to one component type suggests a tray, tape or feeder setup problem.

## Reading a board image
Identify component type, pin geometry and board side first, then look for patterns.

One component type affected suggests a feeder or program error; one board location suggests a head or support error; many similar joints suggest wave direction, clinch or masking. For shadowing, look for dry or thin solder on the side away from the wave. For insertion damage, look for lead deformation, body cracks or board damage at the insertion point. For polarity, compare the component marking with the silkscreen and the pin 1 indicator.

## Match the control to the mechanism
For shadowing, adjust board direction, clinch, stand-off or mask layout before touching wave temperature or speed. For insertion damage, correct feeder, head alignment, pin straightening or board support. For polarity, correct feeder orientation, the vision reference, the program or the manual insertion aid.

Remove the physical cause so the process produces acceptable joints without relying on extra solder or rework.

## Key takeaways
Set board direction and stand-off from the component geometry, because the shadow a body casts is what starves its joint.
Use a controlled clinch that holds the part and still leaves solder a path, since either extreme costs a defect.
Separate polarity and damage from solder defects, because they belong to feeder and insertion control rather than the wave.

## Common mistakes
Raising wave temperature to fill a shadowed joint :: Fix direction, clinch, stand-off or masking, since heat cannot reach behind an obstacle.
Clamping every part tight to the board :: Leave a solder-access gap, or the joint starves under the body.
Treating polarity errors as inspection failures :: Fix feeder and program orientation, and add marking checks to the inspection method.

## Glossary
Shadowing :: A dry or starved joint caused by geometry blocking the solder wave.
Clinch :: Bending the protruding lead to hold the component during handling and soldering.
Stand-off :: The gap between the component body and the board surface.
Dry joint :: A joint that received too little solder to wet and form properly.
