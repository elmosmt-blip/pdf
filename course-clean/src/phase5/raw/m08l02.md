## Plated-hole diameter is the assembly reference
For lead insertion the relevant hole size is the finished plated-hole diameter, not the bare drill diameter. The drill is one input to the stack-up: copper plating, wall roughness and drill wear all reduce the usable diameter.

Measure the finished diameter from the assembly side and the component side, because barrel plating can leave the hole slightly non-cylindrical. A measured diameter close to the lead diameter raises insertion force, skew and fill risk. Check the finished diameter against the fabrication drawing and the board supplier's capability report.

## Lead diameter is a range
Treat the lead diameter as a range, never a single nominal value. Leads may be round, rectangular or formed, and the effective insertion dimension can exceed the nominal wire diameter because of plating, burrs or the forming radius.

Clearance is the finished plated-hole diameter minus the maximum effective lead dimension, and a small nominal clearance can disappear once tolerance stacks up. For production, calculate the worst-case clearance from the minimum plated-hole diameter and the maximum lead dimension, then check it against the insertion equipment's alignment capability and the component datasheet.

## Placement tolerance consumes clearance
Placement tolerance is the positional error between the intended hole centre and the actual lead centre. Feeder repeatability, vision calibration, tape, reel or tube handling, board warpage and lead-forming variation all contribute.

A lead displaced by more than the available radial clearance binds on the hole wall or misses the hole entirely. Manual insertion lets the operator correct some of it; automated insertion has to include placement tolerance in the clearance calculation. Verify the tolerance against the insertion machine specification and the panel datum scheme.

## Clearance is not fill capability
A joint can insert cleanly and still fill badly, because the annular gap is either too small or too large.

Too little clearance traps air, restricts flux flow and stops solder wetting the full wall. Too much lets the lead sit off-centre, opens a void path and weakens capillary control. The desired clearance is therefore a process window, not a number: for wave soldering, the gap must pass solder through the hole while keeping enough capillary action to fill the joint. Verify the acceptable range against the solder process, flux activity, preheat profile and the wave or selective-solder equipment datasheet.

## Expected fill depends on geometry and process
Expected fill is expressed as the percentage of hole depth filled after solidification. Hole aspect ratio, lead-to-hole clearance, surface finish, flux wetting, preheat, solder temperature, dwell time and the soldering method all move it.

A high aspect-ratio hole with tight clearance fills less reliably than a shallow hole with moderate clearance. Use IPC-A-610 acceptance for the applicable product class and confirm actual fill with cross-sections or X-ray where required.

## Stack tolerances before choosing the hole size
A practical stack covers PCB finished-hole tolerance, lead diameter tolerance, lead-forming tolerance, placement tolerance and any allowance for board warpage or panel distortion.

Compare the result with the minimum clearance insertion needs and the minimum gap solder fill needs. A marginal result means a larger hole, a tighter lead tolerance, better placement calibration or a different insertion method, not a hope that nominal values hold. Use worst-case or statistical analysis according to volume and risk.

## Close the loop with measured data
The most reliable clearance and fill predictions come from measured distributions: plated-hole diameter, lead diameter, placement error, and fill results from pilot builds.

If the design clearance sits inside the calculated range and fill is still poor, look at flux activity, preheat, wave height, conveyor speed and hole wall condition. High insertion force points to drill wear, plating thickness, lead burrs or placement repeatability. Record the measured values against the design assumptions so the next hole and lead selection rest on real process capability.

## Key takeaways
Stack every tolerance — hole, lead, forming and placement — before choosing a hole size, because nominal clearance disappears in production.
Treat clearance as a window with two failure ends, since both a tight and a wide gap spoil fill.
Compare pilot-build fill data with the design assumptions, so the next board is designed against measured capability.

## Common mistakes
Choosing the hole from the lead nominal plus a rule of thumb :: Add the tolerances and the placement error, then check both ends of the gap.
Ignoring placement tolerance in an automated line :: Include it in the radial clearance, because the lead centre moves before it reaches the hole.
Fixing poor fill by enlarging the hole alone :: Check flux, preheat and dwell first, since too much clearance also ruins fill.

## Glossary
Tolerance stack-up :: The combined worst-case effect of several independent tolerances on one gap.
Aspect ratio (plated through-hole) :: Hole depth divided by hole diameter, which governs how easily solder fills the barrel.
Insertion force :: The force needed to push a lead into a plated hole, an indicator of clearance and burrs.
Process window :: The range of a parameter that produces acceptable results in production.
