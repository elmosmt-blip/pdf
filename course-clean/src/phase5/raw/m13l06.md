## Product mix and test strategy
Product mix is a test-strategy problem because volume, complexity, cost and the rework loop interact.

High volume favours methods with short cycle time and repeatable contact, since fixture cost spreads over many units and line balance starts to matter. High complexity favours methods that reach dense interconnects, verify presence and value, and isolate faults quickly. Cost pressure favours whatever reduces escapes, rework and test time, which is rarely the cheapest tester on the quotation.

The strategy follows the mix, not the other way round.

## In-circuit test
In-circuit test uses a dedicated fixture with pogo pins to contact the test points.

It is strong on opens, shorts, missing components, wrong values and some solder defects where the net is accessible and the program is well built. Coverage is not the number of test points: it also depends on double-sided access, fine-pitch access, component orientation, and whether power, analogue and high-speed nets are included.

Fixture cost and design lead time are the price of that speed. Verify coverage against the fixture design, the equipment datasheet and the applicable IPC acceptance standard for solder defects.

## Flying probe test
Flying probe replaces the fixture with movable probes that reach test points under program control.

It suits prototypes, low volume, high mix and debug, because fixture cost disappears and program changes come quickly. It gives broad net coverage where test points are accessible, with cycle time usually longer than in-circuit test and probe reach limiting dense or double-sided boards.

Test-point placement and clearance matter more here, because a probe has to land on what the layout gives it.

## Boundary scan
Boundary scan controls device pins through JTAG and tests interconnects without physical probes.

It is valuable where access is limited, test points are scarce, or the interconnects are too dense to probe, and it detects opens, shorts and some device-level faults on scan-capable interconnects, plus programming and debug support.

Its coverage stops at scan-capable devices and scan-capable nets, and non-scan components stay outside it.

## Functional test
Functional test applies power, signals and loads to verify that the assembly behaves as intended.

It catches programming errors, gross functional failures, some marginal defects and system-level interactions the structural tests miss.

It may not isolate the failed net or component, and its coverage depends on the program, the stimulus conditions and the measurement resolution. Functional test is slow and needs specialised fixtures, software and skills, which is why it usually closes the line rather than opens it.

## Choosing a strategy
On high-volume, high-complexity assemblies the common combination is in-circuit test, boundary scan and functional test, which balances coverage, cycle time and rework loop time.

In-circuit test gives fast structural coverage, boundary scan covers where probes cannot reach, and functional test closes the gross functional defects. On low-volume or prototype work, flying probe plus boundary scan plus functional test often beats building a fixture.

## Coverage and uncovered classes
State coverage for nets, components and defect classes, never as one standalone figure.

A strategy can cover most nets and still miss under-component solder defects, non-scan analogue faults, thermal and mechanical defects, intermittent contacts, contamination, programming errors and marginal parametric drift.

Coverage also depends on the conditions: temperature, voltage and timing, because some defects only appear under stress. Name the uncovered classes when the strategy is chosen, and name the control that compensates.

## Rework loop and cost
The rework loop runs from defect detection to corrected unit, and it drives both cost and quality.

A strategy that localises defects quickly shortens diagnosis and stops repeated functional debugging. Structural tests isolate opens, shorts, missing parts and wrong values, while functional tests may only say that a subsystem failed.

Total cost includes fixture, test time, maintenance, program development, escapes, rework and scrap. The cheapest tester is often the expensive choice once the loop is counted.

## Key takeaways
Choose the strategy from volume, complexity and the rework loop, because each driver points at a different method.
State coverage per defect class, since a net percentage says nothing about under-component joints.
Count the rework loop in the cost, because a slow diagnosis turns a cheap tester into an expensive one.

## Common mistakes
Buying a fixture for a high-mix low-volume line :: Use flying probe, because fixture cost cannot spread over a few hundred units.
Reporting coverage as one number :: Break it down by class and condition, because defects live in the gaps.
Leaving functional test to find structural faults :: Cover structure early, because functional failures are the hardest to debug.

## Glossary
High mix :: A production profile with many different assemblies in small quantities.
Rework loop :: The time and cost from detecting a defect to producing a corrected unit.
Pogo pin :: A spring-loaded contact that presses onto a test point in a fixture.
Escape :: A defective unit that passes the strategy and reaches the customer.
