## Why nozzle, pick height and force control matter
On SMT and THT placement, the nozzle, the pick height and the placement force decide whether a component is picked cleanly, placed accurately and seated without mechanical damage.

The nozzle must match component geometry and mass. Pick height sets the vertical distance between nozzle tip and component or board surface. Placement force controls how firmly the part is pressed into paste, a socket, a hole or a seating surface. The three are interdependent: an undersized nozzle slips or crushes a corner, and excessive force cracks ceramic bodies, deforms leads or squeezes paste where it does not belong.

Start from the component supplier's mechanical limits and the machine's nozzle and force capability, then tune inside a narrow window while checking first-article samples and in-process inspection data.

## Nozzle selection by component type
Base nozzle choice on the component top surface, mass, height and tolerance. Chip resistors, capacitors and inductors usually take a vacuum nozzle with a flat or slightly rounded tip that contacts the top surface without covering the terminals.

Larger or heavier parts may need more contact area or a mechanical gripper to stop tipping and slipping. Packages with exposed metal lids, such as some power devices, benefit from a compliant tip or a bigger vacuum area that spreads the point load. THT leads, sockets and connectors may need grippers, push-down tools or guide pins instead of vacuum.

Check nozzle dimensions, vacuum flow and tip material against the placement machine datasheet and the component drawing.

## Pick height and pick quality
Pick height is the vertical position where the nozzle meets the component in the feeder or tray. Too high and the vacuum fails to seal, so the part is missed or picked at an angle. Too low and the nozzle pushes the component into its pocket, damages the feeder or strikes the carrier.

On tape and reel, set pick height so the nozzle just touches the component top with enough travel to establish vacuum without compressing it. On trays, allow for tray thickness, pocket depth and component height variation. A practical starting point is contact plus a small overtravel, often 0.05 mm to 0.20 mm, verified against the machine manual and the feeder supplier's guidance.

## Placement force and mechanical limits
Force must be enough to seat the component and establish paste contact, and low enough to protect component and board. Small passives need little force, because their mass and the paste viscosity do the seating; larger parts may need more to overcome lead springback, connector retention or paste resistance.

Ceramic capacitors, brittle packages and thin leads are damaged by point loads and excess force. The component datasheet should state a maximum placement force or allowable mechanical load; where it does not, use a conservative value and validate it by destructive or non-destructive inspection. Many small SMT components start in the 0.1 N to 1.0 N range, verified against the supplier and the machine.

## Component-specific limits
Failure modes differ by component. Small ceramic capacitors crack under point loading, especially with a worn or misaligned tip. Large MLCCs are sensitive to bending stress during placement and reflow, so force and support area both matter.

Connectors and sockets need enough force to clear retention features and not enough to bend terminals or damage housings. THT parts need insertion force without bending leads, damaging plating or cracking the board. Exposed die, glass or brittle packages forbid narrow nozzles and high point loads, and compliant leads take enough force to seat without flattening them past the supplier's limit.

## Nozzle wear, alignment and monitoring
Nozzle condition is a major defect source. A worn, chipped or contaminated nozzle loses vacuum seal, shifts the pick centre or loads the component unevenly.

Check nozzle alignment on the machine maintenance schedule and inspect tips for wear, residue and diameter change. Trend vacuum pressure and, where available, force feedback to catch drift. On high-reliability products, track nozzle life and replace on measured wear or a validated cycle count, using the machine manual and the nozzle supplier's recommendations.

## Validation and process control
Validate the nozzle, pick height and force with a controlled trial. Start from the component supplier's limits, run a small sample set, and inspect for missed picks, tilted components, tombstoning, paste squeeze-out, lead deformation and component damage.

On critical parts, add cross-sectioning or dye-and-pry to expose hidden cracks. Record nozzle number, pick height, force, vacuum level and any alarms, then lock the recipe and control changes through change management. If the process drifts, check nozzle wear, feeder alignment, tape peel force and board support before touching placement force.

## Key takeaways
Derive pick height from component height, pocket depth and tip length, then prove it on a dry run before production.
Keep placement force between the seating requirement and the component's stated maximum, with the nozzle contact area checked.
Track nozzle wear and vacuum trend, because a degrading tip changes both the pick and the load without any program change.

## Common mistakes
Choosing a nozzle by body size alone :: Check vacuum coverage, tip wear and clearance to neighbouring parts as well.
Raising force to fix a missed paste contact :: Inspect nozzle alignment, paste deposit and board support first, since force is rarely the real limit.
Replacing nozzles on a fixed calendar count :: Replace on measured wear or a validated cycle count, and record the evidence.

## Glossary
Pick height :: The vertical position where the nozzle contacts the component in the feeder or tray.
Overtravel :: The extra downward travel after first contact, which establishes vacuum without compressing the part.
Dye-and-pry :: A destructive test that reveals cracks by forcing dye into them and separating the part.
Point load :: Force concentrated on a small area, which is what causes cracks in brittle components.
