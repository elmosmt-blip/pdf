## Why clearance and fill matter
Through-hole joints live on the interaction between plated hole, component lead and solder process. The finished hole diameter, the lead diameter and the board thickness set the annular space that solder must fill.

A calculation drill shows whether a BOM subset is likely to fill properly before the first build, which is cheaper than finding it out at cross-section.

## Finished hole diameter
The plated-hole diameter is not the drill diameter. Drill size, copper thickness and plating thickness combine to set the finished diameter.

Work from the approved land-pattern library and the fabrication drawing, and verify the finished size against the drill and plating capability. IPC-7351 gives land-pattern and hole-size guidance, and the final value still has to match what fabrication can hold.

## Lead diameter and fit
The component supplier controls the lead diameter, and handling modifies it. Measure at the seating plane and at the exit side where the lead is bent or deformed.

A tight fit blocks capillary flow; a loose fit lets solder drain away and leaves a weak joint. Record nominal and worst-case dimensions rather than leaning on one nominal value.

## Clearance definitions
Clearance is normally evaluated as diametral clearance, the hole diameter minus the lead diameter, with radial clearance at half that.

Both matter: diametral clearance affects assembly and alignment, and radial clearance is the capillary gap that draws solder through the hole. The window is not a design value alone; it must suit the wave or selective solder equipment.

## What drives solder fill
Capillary action, flux activity, preheat and contact time fill the joint. A clean, wettable hole and lead are what let solder climb and fill the annulus.

Preheat too low leaves the hole cold and produces partial fill, and excessive preheat oxidises the joint or dries the flux. Verify preheat and wave settings against your paste and equipment datasheet.

## Acceptance and target
The applicable IPC-A-610 class defines acceptance. IPC-A-610 sets a minimum vertical barrel fill of 75 % for both Class 2 and Class 3, and Class 3 additionally requires visible fillets on the primary and secondary sides.

A documented exception allows about 50 % fill, or 1.2 mm, for parts with fourteen or more leads whose barrels tie into internal thermal or conductor planes. Some customers specify 100 % fill contractually, and that is a customer requirement rather than an IPC-A-610 class limit. Set the process target above the minimum for the required class, and never let the target stand in for the acceptance criteria.

## Calculation approach
For a drill, use nominal dimensions first, then the worst-case tight and loose cases. Calculate annular volume and expected fill volume, then compare the result with the required class and the process capability.

Where expected fill sits close to the minimum, plan cross-section or X-ray verification before production.

## Key takeaways
Compare expected fill with the IPC-A-610 minimum for the required class, and remember Class 3 also needs visible fillets on both sides.
Run the worst-case tight and loose clearances, because the tight case is what stalls capillary flow.
Treat a contractual 100 % fill requirement as a customer requirement and record it separately from the class limit.

## Common mistakes
Assuming the drill diameter is the finished hole :: Use the plated diameter, since plating shrinks the hole the solder must fill.
Quoting 75 % fill as sufficient for every class and contract :: Check Class 3 fillet requirements and any customer specification above the class limit.
Setting a process target and calling it acceptance :: Keep the target above the minimum but judge boards against the standard and the contract.

## Glossary
Diametral clearance :: Hole diameter minus lead diameter at the joint.
Radial clearance :: Half the diametral clearance, the gap on one side of the lead.
Vertical barrel fill :: The percentage of the plated hole depth filled with solder.
Documented exception :: A stated allowance in the standard that permits a lower value under defined conditions.
