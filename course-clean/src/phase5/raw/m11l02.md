## Contact order sets the thermal path
Manual rework works when heat reaches the joint before solder does.

Preheat the board where thermal mass is high, place the tip so it touches lead and pad together, feed solder to the joint, remove the solder, and then remove the tip. That order leaves the interface hot when the solder arrives and keeps cold joints out.

Withdraw the tip too early and the joint solidifies while the operator is still feeding, which gives a disturbed or incomplete fillet. Feed before the interface is hot and the solder balls on the tip or sits on the pad without wetting.

Use thermocouple data on a thick board, a large copper area or a heat-sensitive component, and confirm that the technique suits the joint geometry and the board material.

## Dwell is a process window
Dwell is the time the tip and solder stay in thermal contact with the joint. It must be long enough for lead, pad and solder to reach a wetting condition and short enough to protect board, component and flux.

Dwell follows tip geometry, iron recovery, board thickness, copper area and preheat. A joint on a large copper plane or a long lead needs more heat than a small isolated pad.

Find the minimum dwell that wets on a sacrificial joint, then check it against your paste and equipment datasheet. When wetting is poor, change tip, preheat or technique before extending dwell, because longer contact is not a substitute for better thermal contact and it damages neighbours, darkens flux and weakens the board interface.

## Feed controls volume, flux and bridging
Feed solder wire to the joint, never melt it on the tip.

Wire diameter and feed length should match the joint size, so the operator can place enough solder for a fillet without excess. Flux-cored wire cleans the interface, and too much feed traps flux, creates voids or bridges adjacent pads.

Use short touches and let the joint draw solder into the fillet, watching the flow rather than counting on a remembered amount. A small joint wants a smaller diameter for control; a large joint can take a larger one, still fed to the joint and never flooded over the area. Check diameter, flux activity and technique against your paste and equipment datasheet.

## Lead-free discipline
SAC-type lead-free solder needs higher wetting temperatures and more active flux than tin-lead, and it forgives less: contamination, mixed alloys and a poor tip condition all show up faster.

Dedicated tips and clean work areas limit cross-contamination. Choose setpoints and dwell from the solder or paste datasheet, then confirm on the actual board.

Lead-free discipline also controls the thermal history of the joint, because repeated rework changes wetting behaviour and intermetallic formation. Never share a tip between tin-lead and lead-free work unless the process is qualified and the tip cleaned, and document the alloy and flux system for each rework task.

## Iron and tip must match the joint
The iron has to recover temperature during contact, and the tip has to transfer heat without blocking inspection or forcing the operator to press.

A fine tip suits small SMD joints, and a chisel or hoof tip with real contact area suits through-hole joints. Match the tip face to the lead and pad geometry.

An undersized tip cools during contact and pushes the operator into a longer dwell; an oversized one damages nearby parts and is hard to control. Check tip condition at the start of every shift, because oxidation, wear and poor plating cut heat transfer and wetting, and confirm with calibration records that the displayed temperature matches the measured one.

## Technique defects are thermal or mechanical
Insufficient wetting usually comes from a low interface temperature, short dwell, an oxidised tip or contaminated surfaces.

Disturbed joints happen when the component moves while the solder solidifies. Bridging and excess solder come from overfeeding or poor tip withdrawal. Lifted pads follow excessive dwell, high preheat or mechanical prying. Voids come from trapped flux, moisture or heating fast enough to drive volatiles through the joint, and burned flux leaves residue that hampers inspection and may reduce corrosion resistance.

Inspect against the IPC acceptance standard and correct the process parameter that caused the defect rather than touching up repeatedly.

## State the thermal budget before rework
A thermal budget is the controlled combination of tip setpoint, board preheat, contact time, number of contacts, tip geometry and alloy.

Write it down for each rework task so another engineer can reproduce the result. Measure board temperature with a thermocouple where the joint is thermally significant, and record the values actually used rather than the nominal setpoint.

Align the budget with the IPC acceptance standard and the solder, paste and equipment datasheets. State whether the joint was repaired with an initial contact or whether more contacts were allowed, and define the maximum board temperature the component tolerates. That makes the technique auditable and stops technique defects becoming routine.

## Key takeaways
Heat the joint before feeding solder, because the interface temperature decides whether the solder wets or balls.
Find the minimum dwell that wets and stop there, since extra contact damages the board without improving the fillet.
Write the thermal budget down, so the next engineer reproduces the joint instead of guessing at it.

## Common mistakes
Melting solder on the tip to carry it to the joint :: Feed to the joint, because solder on the tip arrives without a wetted interface.
Extending dwell when a joint will not wet :: Change tip, preheat or technique first, since dwell is already the expensive variable.
Recording only the iron setpoint in the budget log :: Record measured tip and board temperature, because the display can drift from reality.

## Glossary
Disturbed joint :: A joint whose component moved while the solder was solidifying, leaving a rough surface.
Thermal budget :: The written combination of setpoint, preheat, contact time and alloy allowed for a rework.
Flux-cored wire :: Solder wire with a flux core that cleans the interface as it melts.
Interface temperature :: The temperature at the boundary between solder and the surfaces being joined.
