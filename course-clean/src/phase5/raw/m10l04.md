## What a selective program controls
A selective soldering program is a machine-readable recipe for one board, not a list of coordinates.

It defines where flux lands, how long the board preheats, how the air knife behaves, which joints go first, how the nozzle moves, how long each joint dwells, how many passes run, and how the board cools before handling.

The inputs are board geometry, component list, pad and hole dimensions, copper area, component height, board thickness, fixture design, flux type, alloy, nozzle temperature and machine limits. The output is a repeatable cycle that wets properly without excess heat, flux carryover, bridging or mechanical disturbance.

Each path, dwell, pass and fixture decision should trace to a board feature or a measured thermal result, so another engineer can audit the program and reproduce it on the same equipment, fixture and material set.

## Path planning under the board
Path planning starts from the bottom side, because selective soldering normally reaches through-hole pins and bottom-side pads from below.

On a through-hole joint the path centres on the hole or pad and approaches from a direction that drags no solder across neighbouring pads. A continuous wave path works for pin rows when nozzle width, flux coverage and pad spacing allow it; isolated joints are usually safer point to point, which limits solder dragged into nearby gaps. Bottom-side surface-mount pads follow the pad geometry and avoid crossing between pads unless the method is qualified.

Keep lead-in and lead-out short, predictable and outside the final wetted area. Account for shadowing from tall components, connectors and shielding cans, and verify nozzle-to-board, nozzle-to-component and pin-to-support clearance on the first article rather than assuming them.

## Ordering joints for heat and flux
The order of joints is a thermal decision. Group by access and thermal mass, then run from low-mass to high-mass joints while the board is still warm from preheat.

High-mass joints such as ground-plane pins and heavy connectors need more energy, and placing them too early cools the nozzle or wave; placing them too late overheats the board near sensitive parts. Avoid scheduling a high-mass joint immediately before one near a heat-sensitive component unless a cooling pause or fixture airflow is available.

Grouping also cuts travel: joints sharing a row, a connector footprint or a nozzle approach run together, and grouping must not force the nozzle across completed joints or drag solder over unprocessed pads. Use parallel fluxing or preheat only after confirming that flux activation, board temperature and wetting stay inside the qualified window. Document the reason for each group, because later troubleshooting needs to know whether a defect came from path, thermal mass or sequence.

## Fixtures
The fixture is part of the program: it sets board height, exposure, support and thermal behaviour.

A good fixture holds the board flat, exposes the bottom side, supports areas that would flex, and blocks neither nozzle, fluxer nor air knife. Insulating, low-heat-transfer materials such as PEEK or phenolic are common for temperature stability and stiffness.

Vacuum hold-down helps, and vacuum holes must not pull flux into unwanted areas or seat the board unevenly. Support pins sit under large components, connector bodies and board edges, not so close to through-hole pads that they block solder access. Pin height sets the board-to-nozzle relationship, so a change in board thickness may need a z-height change even when the coordinates stay put.

The fixture should drain flux and stop it pooling on the top side, and mixed-technology boards may need cutouts or shields against flux mist and radiant heat. Check flatness, pin wear and seating on the first article, because a small height error changes dwell contact and either starves the joint or floods it.

## Air knife and flux control
The air knife is a programmed air or nitrogen curtain that strips excess flux from the board surface and, on some machines, limits flux carryover toward the nozzle or pot. It controls how much flux reaches the joint.

Too little leaves excess flux that boils during soldering and causes blowback, voids or dross. Too much cools the joint, disturbs small components, oxidises the wetting surface or dries the flux before it activates.

Define whether the knife runs, when it fires relative to fluxing and soldering, its pressure, distance and angle. Starting ranges are 20-60 kPa, 5-15 mm and 15-45 degrees, verified against the paste, flux and equipment datasheet. Dry filtered air is common; nitrogen serves where oxidation control matters, with gas purity and flow qualified.

Check for clogging, uneven flow and leakage, because a partly blocked knife leaves a local flux imbalance that shows up as a random wetting defect. In troubleshooting, compare knife settings with defect location: defects near the flux application edge usually point to flux volume or knife timing rather than dwell.

## Dwell and pass strategy
Dwell is how long the solder source stays in contact with, or close enough to, the joint. Set it from thermal mass, pad size, hole size, lead material, flux activity and nozzle temperature.

Small pins and small bottom-side pads need less dwell than ground-plane pins or heavy connector leads. A practical starting range is 0.8-2.5 s for low-mass joints and 1.5-4.0 s for high-mass joints, with the qualified value coming from thermocouple data and cross-sections.

On high-mass joints, several short passes usually beat one long dwell, because heat spreads without overheating neighbours. Include cooling between passes when board temperature climbs too fast, and record the pass count with its reason: one pass for simple joints, two or more for high copper area, thick leads or a poor wetting history.

Never raise dwell to compensate for low preheat, thin flux coverage or a wrong nozzle height. A joint wanting excessive dwell points to path, fixture height, flux volume or the thermal profile, and those come first.

## Cycle time, verification and prevention
Cycle time sums board handling, flux application, preheat, air-knife action, solder movement, dwell, cooling and fixture exchange. Calculate it from the program, then verify it on the machine.

Optimise by cutting non-value-added travel, grouping joints logically, and using machine features only where they preserve wetting. Never shorten preheat or cooling for throughput unless the profile and acceptance criteria still hold.

Verification covers first-article inspection, thermocouples at representative joints, cross-sections on critical joints and acceptance against IPC-A-610. Rework follows IPC-7711/7721 rather than improvised extra heat cycles.

Prevention is built into the program: path geometry prevents bridging, order prevents heat damage, fixture support prevents flex, air-knife control prevents flux carryover, and dwell and pass strategy prevent cold joints. Version-control the program with board revision, fixture revision, flux lot, nozzle condition and measured cycle time, so a good run can be compared with a bad one and the difference traced to material, fixture wear, nozzle condition or settings.

## Key takeaways
Trace every path, dwell and pass back to a board feature or a thermal measurement, so the program can be audited.
Keep lead-in and lead-out outside the wetted area, because that is where dragged solder bridges neighbours.
Record the pass count with its reason, since a later investigation needs to know whether the joint needed the extra heat or the extra time.

## Common mistakes
Raising dwell to fix a cold joint :: Check preheat, flux coverage and nozzle height first, since dwell is the most expensive lever.
Using vacuum hold-down without checking the holes :: Confirm flux cannot be pulled into unwanted areas and the board seats evenly.
Optimising cycle time by shortening cooling :: Keep the profile and acceptance criteria intact, or the boards leave too hot to handle.

## Glossary
Lead-in and lead-out :: The non-soldering travel into and away from a joint.
Air knife :: A timed air or nitrogen curtain that removes excess flux from the board surface.
Vacuum hold-down :: A fixture method that pulls the board flat against a support surface.
Version control :: Keeping each program revision tied to the board, fixture and material set it was qualified with.
