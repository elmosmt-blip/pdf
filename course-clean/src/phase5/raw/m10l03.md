## Thermal budget as a shared resource
The thermal budget is more than the target joint profile: it is the cumulative heat reaching nearby parts by conduction, convection and radiation.

In reflow, wave and hand soldering, the board, copper planes, leads, fixtures and air all carry heat. A neighbour can pass its datasheet limit while the target joint sits comfortably inside specification.

## Dominant heat-transfer paths
Conduction through copper planes and component leads usually dominates close to the heat source. Convection from oven air or wave flux vapour sets the background temperature. Radiation matters less at soldering temperatures, though hot fixtures or a solder pot can contribute.

The path with the lowest thermal resistance controls the peak temperature the neighbour sees, so that is the path to interrupt.

## What raises neighbour exposure
Large copper areas, thick board material, high-conductivity substrates, dense placement and low standoff carry heat further.

High paste volume, long time above liquidus, high peak and slow cooling extend the exposure in time. A fixture touching the board either sinks heat or traps it, depending on material and contact area.

## Sensitive neighbours
Parts that need watching include electrolytic capacitors, plastic connectors, LEDs, MEMS devices, sensors, adhesive-bonded components and anything with a moisture sensitivity level.

Their limits are usually a maximum body temperature, a maximum time at temperature, a moisture sensitivity class and a limit on mechanical deformation. Set the allowable budget from the component datasheet and the assembly process specification.

## Estimation approach
Early in layout, estimate neighbour exposure from the heat source temperature and an attenuation factor based on distance, copper connectivity and local thermal mass.

For production, replace the estimate with thermocouple profiling at the worst-case location. The estimate screens; the profile is the evidence.

## Mitigation
Mitigate at layout, process or fixture level.

Layout options are more spacing, thermal relief, less copper connectivity and moving sensitive parts away from heat sources. Process options are a lower peak, a shorter time above liquidus, selective soldering, less paste and an adjusted conveyor speed. Fixtures and shields cut radiant or convective heat, and heat sinks pull heat away.

## Verification
Verify with a thermal profile recorded at the target joint and at the most exposed neighbour, plus functional test after exposure and inspection for deformation, discolouration or solder defects.

Record the profile, the thermocouple locations and the acceptance criteria. Where a neighbour sits near its limit, repeat the profile after any change to paste, oven setup, fixture or component supplier.

## Key takeaways
Place thermocouples on the neighbour as well as the joint, because the joint can pass while the neighbour fails.
Interrupt the lowest-resistance path, since that is what sets the neighbour's peak rather than distance alone.
Re-profile after any fixture, paste or supplier change, because the assumption that produced the old margin has moved.

## Common mistakes
Watching the pot temperature as a proxy for neighbour temperature :: Measure the body, since attenuation and thermal mass change the number entirely.
Relying on a layout estimate for release :: Profile the production board, because the estimate only screens.
Accepting a neighbour close to its limit :: Keep margin and repeat the profile after any process change.

## Glossary
Attenuation length :: The distance over which the temperature rise from a source falls by a factor of e.
Thermal resistance :: How strongly a path resists heat flow; the lowest-resistance path carries the most heat.
Moisture sensitivity level :: The J-STD-020 class that limits how much thermal exposure a package may take.
Thermal shield :: A physical barrier that reduces radiant or convective heat reaching a part.
