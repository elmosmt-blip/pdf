## Purpose of the evidence pack
An evidence pack is a compact set of records showing what the SMT or through-hole process is actually doing, not what it was intended to do.

For a working process engineer it lets another engineer or a manager answer three questions without visiting the line: what is being measured, what the physical evidence shows, and what the financial consequence of the defect is.

If the pack cannot answer those three, it is a folder rather than evidence.

## Measured data that belongs in the pack
Measured data covers both process parameters and output quality.

Typical inputs are stencil thickness, aperture area, paste volume, reflow peak temperature, time above liquidus, placement accuracy and oven profile repeatability. Typical outputs are joint quality, defect counts, first-pass yield, rework count and scrap count.

For each parameter record the unit, the measurement method, the sample size, the time window and the acceptance limit, because a number without those is not comparable to anything.

## Images with scale and context
An image is evidence only when it can be interpreted later.

Each image carries a scale reference: a calibrated ruler, a scale bar or a known component dimension, plus the magnification or field of view. The file name or caption identifies board, panel, station, defect type, date and shift.

For solder defects, take a close-up and a wider view showing the component, adjacent pads and the surrounding land, because the close-up alone cannot show where it sits. Include the stencil side, the board side and, where it matters, a 3D measurement or a cross-section.

## Defect rate and defect density
Define the defect rate clearly.

A common board-level metric is defects per million opportunities, and the opportunity count must be stated: one joint, one pad, one component or one inspection point.

On surface-mount work, defect density is often more useful than a pass percentage, because it compares across board sizes and mixes where a simple percentage does not.

## Sampling and time window
A single shift misleads when the line runs a mixed product or a changeover happened.

State the sampling window: one shift, one day, one week, one production order. For a process comparison, use matched conditions — same product, same line, same paste lot family, same stencil, similar board mix.

Where matched conditions are impossible, state the differences and treat the comparison as indicative rather than conclusive.

## Cost of poor quality
Cost of poor quality includes scrap, rework labour, inspection labour, line downtime, expedited freight, warranty or field failure exposure, and lost capacity.

For a line-level estimate start with direct costs: scrapped boards, rework time, additional inspection time and machine time consumed by rework. For a product-level estimate add downstream test failures, repair and customer returns.

Keep the calculation transparent enough that someone can check one of the terms.

## Linking evidence to process impact
The pack connects data, images, defect rate and cost into one argument.

For insufficient solder, show the measured paste volume distribution, images of the affected joints, the defect rate by station or product, and the rework cost per affected board.

The conclusion states the impact in operational terms: yield loss, throughput loss, rework burden, or risk of escape.

## Presentation and traceability
The pack can be a short report, a spreadsheet or a slide set, and it must be traceable.

Include a cover page with product name, line, date range, author and revision, attach the raw data or give the path where it is stored, and use consistent units, preferably SI: millimetres, micrometres, grams, seconds and degrees Celsius.

For images, embed captions in the report and keep the original files with their metadata.

## Key takeaways
Build the pack so it answers what is measured, what the evidence shows and what it costs, because those three questions decide whether anyone acts.
Put the unit and the window with every number, since a bare figure cannot be compared with the next build.
Keep images interpretable later, because a close-up without scale is an anecdote.

## Common mistakes
Reporting a defect rate without the opportunity count :: State what is counted, because a joint and a board give different numbers for the same line.
Comparing two shifts running different products :: Match conditions or label the comparison as indicative, since product mix dominates the rate.
Quoting a cost of poor quality with no terms :: Show the build-up, because a total nobody can check is not persuasive.

## Glossary
Defects per million opportunities :: A defect rate normalised by the number of countable opportunities in the product.
Defect density :: Defects normalised to board area or joint count, allowing comparison across sizes.
Cost of poor quality :: The cost of scrap, rework, inspection and lost capacity caused by defects.
Matched conditions :: A comparison where product, line, materials and process settings are held comparable.
